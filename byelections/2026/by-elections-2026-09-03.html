Chesterton and Launton

Oxfordshire county council; caused by the resignation of Liberal Democrat councillor William Boucher-Giles. We now travel south into England for a division which covers a large swathe of north-eastern Oxfordshire. The Chesterton and Launton county division contains a total of 17 parishes, surrounding the town of Bicester on three sides. The two eponymous villages can be found close to Bicester on opposite sides of the town. To the west is Chesterton, which takes its name from its location on the Roman road of Akeman Street towards Cirencester: this is followed by the modern-day Green Lane through the village, which continues west in a straight line to Kirtlington. Launton is lcoated east of Bicester on the railway line towards Bletchley, which has recently been reinstated as part of the East West Rail project; however, passenger services on this route have yet to restart and might not include the reinstatement of Launton railway station, which closed in 1967. Further new railway construction is underway at the north-east corner of this division in the parishes of Finmere, Godington, Mixbury and Newton Purcell with Shelswell, which lie on the route of High Speed 2. Infrastructure of an earlier age can be found in the M40 motorway, which runs through the western part of the division. Here can be found the Cherwell Valley service area, which was opened in 1994 and rebuilt after being destroyed by fire in 2010. Dsspite the name, this service area is not in the Cherwell valley at all: the brook which runs next to the services is a tributary of the River Great Ouse and lies outside the Thames catchment area. Launton is one of only two parishes in this division with more than 1,000 electors on the roll. Despite the division name, the largest parish here is Caversfield which is now essentially a northern extension of Bicester; this village has always had a special status, as it was a detached part of Buckinghamshire until 1844. Caversfield has had a long association with the military thanks to the presence of nearby Bicester Aerodrome, which lies over the parish boundary in Launton: this was an RAF grass airfield until 2004, latterly used for glider training, and it is now run by a firm called Bicester Motion as a business park dedicated to motoring and aviation. Clients include the Audi Formula 1 team, who have a technology centre here. Vorsprung durch Technik, as some people might say. The Bicester area in general has seen strong population growth this century, and the sort of people who have moved here have shown willingness to vote for the Liberal Democrats in recent years. Bicester joined the parliamentary map in 2024 with the creation of the Bicester and Woodstock constituency, which was won at its first contest by the Liberal Democrats' Calum Miller; he is now the party's foreign affairs spokesman, and he had previously been a senior civil servant in the Bernard Woolley role of principal private secretary. Chesterton and Launton county division was created by a redistribution in 2025 which gave extra seats to Bicester and the surrounding area, and it turned in quite a close result. William Boucher-Giles was elected as the Lib Dem county councillor with just 33% of the vote thanks to a perfect right-wing vote split, with the Conservatives polling 28% and Reform UK 27%. The main predecessor divisions of Ploughley and Bicester North had both been strongly Conservative at the previous county elections in 2021. The Lib Dem win here was important, as the party only narrowly won control of Oxfordshire county council in 2025 with 36 seats against 12 Labour, 10 Conservative, 7 Greens and 1 each for Reform UK, the Independent Oxford Alliance (who caucus with the Conservatives), other independents (who also caucus with the Conservatives) and the Henley Residents Group. One Lib Dem county councillor has since quit the party, so the Lib Dems need to hold this by-election to keep their overall majority on the council. The local authority here is Cherwell council, which combines the Bicester area with the Banbury area north of Oxford. The ward boundaries don't match up, but this division has similar boundaries to the Fringford and Heyfords ward which was a Lib Dem gain from the Conservatives in 2024 and 2026. This ward doesn't include Caversfield and Launton parishes, which are also in wards which voted Lib Dem in 2026 (respectively Bicester North and Caversfield, and Launton and Otmoor). This crucial by-election has been triggered by the resignation of William Boucher-Giles, who has relocated to Switzerland with his family. Defending for the Lib Dems is James Hartley, a Royal Navy veteran who has been a regular council candidate elsewhere in the Cherwell district in recent years: he has contested Deddington ward at each election from 2022 to 2026. The Conservatives' Sandy Dallimore stood unsuccessfully here in 2025 and in Fringford and Heyfords in May: he is a former Cherwell councillor who represented Bicester East ward from 2021 to 2024. Also back for another go after standing unsuccessfully here in 2025 is Reform UK's Ian Hodgson, who stood in Launton and Otmoor ward in May and runs the party's Bicester and Woodstock branch. Completing this ballot paper are Meri Mosharrafa for the Greens and Gregor Fish for Labour. Llantwit Fardre

Rhondda Cynon Taf council; caused by the resignation of Conservative councillor Sam Trask. Our Welsh by-election this week in in the county borough of Rhondda Cynon Taf, which predominantly covers the Valleys of south Wales: the three rivers in the name are a bit of a clue. But Llantwit Fardre doesn't fit neatly into any of the valleys, because it lies on relatively high ground between the Taff to the east and the Ely to the west. The main communication link here is a relatively new link road, opened in 2010 to relieve the congested Llantrisant Road through Llantwit Fardre, which bypasses the village and provides a fast connection from the Taff valley to Llantrisant. This road separates Llantwit Fardre from the smaller village of Efail Isaf to the west. The good communication links and the area's proximity to Cardiff have turned Llantwit Fardre into a commuter centre, with voting patterns to match. In the 2008 local elections Llabtwit Fardre became the first ward of Rhondda Cynon Taf to return a Conservative councillor, Joel James, and at the most recent Welsh local elections in 2022 this area remained the only splash of blue on the local electoral map as the Tory slate defeated Labour by 49-35. The only other RCT ward the Conservatives have ever won was Pont-y-clun in 2017, but that ward was broken up by a boundary review in 2022. Looking up to voting patterns in the two Parliaments confirms that Llantwit Fardre is not typical of the wider area. At Westminster level the village is part of the Pontypridd constituency, which has been in Labour hands for over a century. The current MP Alex Davies-Jones has represented the seat since 2019, and she is on the Ministry of Justice's frontbench team with responsibility for victims, violence against women and girls, and international justice. In May's Senedd elections the wider constituency of Pontypridd Cynon Merthyr returned three MSs for Plaid Cymru, two for Reform UK and one for Labour; top of the Plaid list was Heledd Fychan, who is now in the Welsh Cabinet as Trefnydd (or leader of the Senedd), Plaid chief whip and cabinet minister for culture and sport. May's result suggests that the Conservatives have some work to do in this by-election. The outgoing councillor is Sam Trask, who won a previous by-election here in May 2021 and was subsequently selected as the Conservatives' prospective candidate for Bridgend in the 2024 general election - only to pull out at the last minute following a controversy over sexually crude social media posts about women. Trask has now stood down from Rhondda Cynon Taf council because he has moved away from the area. In the 2022 local elections he had beaten his Conservative running-mate Karl Johnson who was higher up the alphabetical ballot paper, which might suggest that Trask had developed something of a personal vote. Defending this seat for the Conservatives is Joel James, who back in 2008 had become the first Conservative Rhondda Cynon Taf councillor when he was elected for this ward. James was elected in 2021 to the Senedd as a regional MS for South Wales Central and retired from the council in 2022 in consequence, but he failed to be re-elected as an MS in May when he was top of the Conservative list in Caerdydd Ffynnon Taf. Another defeated Senedd candidate from May on the ballot is Labour's Lloyd Watkins, who was number 2 on their list for Pontypridd Cynon Merthyr; Watkins is a former civil servant who has a PhD on the impact of artificial intelligence, robotics and automation on government. Also on an all-male ballot paper are Plaid Cymru's Danny Crudge who gives an address in the ward, serial independent candidate Jonthan Bishop whose long recent electoral record includes finishing in ninth and last place in this ward in 2017 and in the Pontypridd constituency in 2024, and Reform UK's Lyndon Evans who is described as retired with a professional background in commerce and logistics. Frome West

Somerset council; caused by the disqualification of Green Party councillor Michael Dunk. Our last by-election this week is a controversial one. It stems from the actions of Just Stop Oil, a protest group which was active from 2022 to 2025 on the issue of man-made climate change and net zero carbon emissions. This is a topic which, in general, has broad support from the UK public. Polling from YouGov in November 2025 suggested that 60% of Britons supported the UK government's commitments to cutting carbon emissions and achieving net zero by 2050. Much work has already been done towards this target. The UK's wind and solar power generation increases year on year as fossil fuels become unable to compete with clean energy on price; electric cars make up a growing proportion of the UK's transport fleet. But there is a lot which remains to be done on this subject in the years to come. The broad popular support for net zero has been achieved despite the actions of Just Stop Oil, which did its best to bring the environmental cause into disrepute with a series of high-profile, dangerous and illegal publicity stunts. These included a series of events in which Just Stop Oil protestors climbed gantries on the M25 London orbital motorway between 7th and 11th November 2022, forcing the motorway to be closed because of the danger to members of the public caused by their actions. The disruption caused by this to people trying to go about their daily lives as normal was widespread. One of the protestors involved was Michael Dunk, who scaled an M25 gantry on 8th November 2022. Dunk was subsequently charged with intentionally or recklessly causing a public nuisance, contrary to section 78 of the Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022. This offence is aimed at conduct which - to quote the Act's explanatory notes - endangers the life, health, property or comfort of a section of the public or obstructs them in the exercise of their rights. It carries a maximum sentence of ten years' imprisonment. Section 78 of the 2022 Act had been passed as the result of a Law Commission recommendation from 2015 to replace the previous common law offence of "public nuisance", for which the maximum sentence was theoretically unlimited. In October 2025 Dunk went on trial at Southwark Crown Court alongside five other Just Stop Oil protestors, all of whom were found guilty by the jury. As in earlier cases involving the November 2022 M25 protests, the trial judge proceeded to throw the book at the defendants. When Judge Perrins passed sentence in December 2025, Michael Dunk was sentenced to 2 years and 2 months in prison and ordered to pay court costs of £4,380. This sentence also disqualifies him from serving as a councillor for five years, but the disqualification did not take effect immediately because Dunk appealed against his conviction. Dunk was eventually ejected from Somerset council in July after the Court of Appeal rejected his appeal; in the interim he had avoided disqualification under the six-month non-attendance rule by attending council meetings while on early release from prison. This finally allows the Frome West by-election to go ahead. This is one of three Somerset wards covering the town of Frome, with West ward taking in the town centre together with a small rural hinterland to the west of the town which is part of the parish of Selwood. Frome is unusual for eastern Somerset in being an old industrial town, with textiles being the traditional industry here. It was enfranchised by the Great Reform Act in 1832, at which point the town had a population of around 11,000 with significant population growth expected as a result of the Industrial Revolution: but this never happened, and the town was disenfranchised in 1885. Today Frome's population is around 29,000. The town's time as an independent parliamentary borough did not get off to a promising start. In the 1832 election only 322 people in Frome had the right to vote, and they had a choice of two candidates: Sir Thomas Champneys (later Mostyn-Champneys) and Thomas Sheppard. There was a lot of bad blood between them. Champneys was the squire of nearby Orchardleigh, but he was in debt and neglected his estate; he was also unmarried, and his reputation was such that in 1820 he had stood trial for sodomy, ultimately being found not guilty. He also owned slaves, which in 1832 was still legal but seriously controversial. Sheppard was firmly on the anti-slavery side, and he had been a key prosecution witness in Champneys' 1820 sodomy trial. The two days of voting in Frome were marred by serious violence, most of which came from Champneys' supporters: a Sheppard supporter died, a house was destroyed, the Riot Act was read out, and the Army had to be called in to restore order. The poll was terminated as soon as Sheppard had secured enough votes to mathematically ensure his win: he was declared the victor by 163 votes to 100, and went on to serve for four terms as MP for Frome.

Sheppard's successor, Robert Boyle, was unseated in 1852 by an election petition. The Commons committee which ruled on election petitions in those days decided that he was disqualified by virtue of being secretary of the Most Illustrious Order of St Patrick, the Irish equivalent of the Order of the Garter. (The last Knight of St Patrick was the old Duke of Gloucester who died in 1974, but the Order has never been formally abolished and Charles III remains its Sovereign.) Boyle resigned his secretaryship and won the resulting Frome by-election unopposed. He died the following year from disease while serving in the Crimean War, and the resulting by-election returned his older brother Richard Boyle, Viscount Dungarvan. Richard succeeded to his father's titles in 1856 and entered the Lords as the 9th Earl of Cork and Orrery; the resulting second Frome by-election returned yet another brother, William Boyle, who defeated the Radical candidate Donald Nicoll by one vote, 158 to 157. Nicoll got his revenge in the general election the following year.

The borough's electorate was small enough that pressure could be brought to bear on the voters by the local aristocrats. In Frome's case that meant the Marquess of Bath, whose seat at Longleat is just over over the county boundary in Wiltshire. A number of members of the Bath family have served as MP for Frome, starting with Lord Edward Thynne who defeated Donald Nicoll to serve as a Conservative MP here from 1859 to 1865. In 1868 Frome had a celebrity MP: the Liberals' Thomas Hughes was a barrister who was prominent in the co-operative movement, but he is remembered today primarily for turning his education at Rugby School into the bestselling novel Tom Brown's School Days.

Since 1885 Frome has been included within a number of Somerset county seats with a variety of boundaries: for example, the 1918-50 Frome constituency included much of what are now Bristol's southern suburbs and as a result it saw strong population growth during that period. The present constituency of Frome and East Somerset was created for the 2024 general election and returned Liberal Democrat candidate Anna Sabine, who is now the party's spokeswoman for culture, media and sport. This seat had been created as a split of the previous Somerton and Frome constituency, which had been gained by the Lib Dems in a July 2023 by-election after the previous Conservative MP David Warburton was brought down by a cocaine and misconduct scandal. The 2023 Somerton and Frome by-election saw the Green Party candidate Martin Dimery finish in third place with 10.2% of the vote, which was at the time a record for the Greens in a parliamentary by-election. Much of this vote will have come out of Frome town, which now has a strong "alternative" vibe and has given high votes to Green candidates in local elections for some years now. In the 2022 Somerset council elections four of the five Green seats came out of Frome, with Dimery and Michael Dunk winning the two seats in Frome West with a 47-33 lead over the Lib Dems. These boundaries had previously been used for elections to Somerset county council from 2013, and the Greens had gained Frome West from the Lib Dems in the 2017 election; on that occasion the outgoing Lib Dem county councillor Derek Tanswell sought re-election on the ticket of the continuing Liberal Party and clearly thought he was sufficiently on first-name terms with the electorate to appear on the ballot paper mononymously as "Derek". He finished in fifth and last place with 2.7%. Before his election to the present Somerset council in 2022, Michael Dunk had previously represented Frome Market ward on Mendip council from 2019 until that council was abolished in 2023. Defending this Somerset council by-election for the Green Party is Dominic Ellison, who is the chief executive of a charity providing services and support to disabled people and their families in the West of England. The Liberal Democrats' Drew Gardner has previously served on Mendip council, representing Frome College ward from 2019 until abolition in 2023. Another all-male ballot is completed by David Quantrill-Simon for the Conservatives, Christopher Wildridge for Labour and Reform UK's Joseph Williams.
