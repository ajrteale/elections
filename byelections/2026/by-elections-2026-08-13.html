Three by-elections on 13th August 2026, and we start with the Parliamentary Special:

Clacton

House of Commons, Essex; caused by the resignation of Reform UK MP Nigel Farage. The Clacton parliamentary seat was only created in 2010, but this area's position in the north-east corner of Essex has generally made the constituency map here stable over the decades and centuries. There is effectively only one predcessor seat covering the whole of the period since 1885, when single-member constituencies became the norm for UK parliamentary elections: but until 2010 that seat took its name from an older town which is no longer part of this constituency. The port of Harwich had been enfranchised in 1604, entitling it to send two members to Parliament who were elected by the borough corporation until the First Reform Act of 1832. In practice its elections were controlled by the government and particularly by the Treasury, which had a major influence in Harwich thanks to its control over lucrative jobs in the town's custome house and post office, and as a result Harwich has more than its fair share of notable MPs. The diarist Samuel Pepys represented the town briefly in 1679 and again from 1685 to 1689, while he was secretary of the Admiralty. From 1807 to 1826 one of Harwich's two seats was held successively by Tories William Huskisson, a long-serving and high-profile statesman who is now best remembered for being killed by a train on the opening day of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway in 1830; then by Nicholas Vansittart, whose service as Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1812 to 1823 has since been beaten only by Gladstone (who had four separate stints in the job); and then by the foreign secretary George Canning, who was subsequently prime minister for a period of approximately 2.4 Liz Trusses. A series of elections here in the late 1840s and early 1850s were voided for bribery and corruption, and after two failed Harwich by-elections in 1851 Parliament refused to issue a writ for a third by-election for almost a year, effectively leaving one of the town's seats vacant as punishment for bad behaviour. Having been reduced to one seat by the Second Reform in 1868, Harwich was disenfranchised by the Third Reform in 1885 but its name was transferred to one the new county division covering the north-east corner of Essex. The 1885 Harwich constituency contained the whole of what is now Tendring district plus the rural area surrounding Colchester, which was the only one of the three ancient Essex parliamentary boroughs left standing by the redistribution. Nonetheless Essex came out of the 1885 boundary changes as a winner, with the creation of two new county seats and the enfranchisement of West Ham ensuring that the county went up from 10 MPs to 11. The reorganised Harwich seat was won at its first contest in 1885 by Conservative James Round, who came from a prominent local landowning family and had represented the predecessor seat of East Essex since 1868. Round was one of the select group of MPs who had played first-class cricket, and he turned out in a total of 22 first-class matches for Oxford and MCC in the 1860s; he also played for Essex in the days before Essex was a first-class county. James Round's first-class career batting average was 16.85, but he had better success at the ballot box where he never lost an election; his closest call came in the 1892 general election where his majority over the Liberals was 305. Nobody opposed Round's final re-election in 1900, and he was sworn of the Privy Council in 1902 as a reward for his long service in the House of Commons. James Round retired at the 1906 general election after 40 years on the green benches. His Harwich seat then fell in the Liberal landslide to Arthur Lever, a former Royal Fusiliers officer who had retired from the Army with the rank of major. Lever's majority over the Conservative candidate Harry Newton was 342 votes. This narrow margin wasn't sustainable when the Liberal landslide subsided. A rematch between Lever and Newton in January 1910 resulted in a win for Newton by over 1,000 votes, and Harry Newton went on to serve as MP for Harwich for 12 years. Newton had been called to the Bar, but his father Sir Alfred Newton, 1st Baronet, was a Hull-born businessman who had become a City financier and chairman of the board of Harrods. Newton senior was also involved in City politics and he rose to become Lord Mayor of London in 1899-1900, the year that the Boer War broke out; his contribution to this was to establish the City of London Imperial Volunteers to fight in South Africa, and young Harry went with them. In 1921 Harry Newton succeeded to his father's titles and became Sir Harry Newton, 2nd Baronet, in controversial circumstances. Sir Alfred Newton had suddenly died of a seizure at Harrods, and the police investigation found that he had taken prescribed indigestion medicine from a bottle which contained enough strychnine to kill several people. The medicine bottle had been made up by the Harrods pharmacist. After the inquest jury had heard evidence from the pharmacist about her working practices and seen a post-mortem report which indicated that Alfred Newton's heart was in poor condition, the coroner recorded a verdict of death by heart failure. Sir Harry Newton retired as MP for Harwich in 1922, and again the Conservatives failed to pass the seat on to their new candidate. The Harwich constituency had been redrawn in 1918 with the same boundaries as the modern Tendring district, and it returned the Liberal Party candidate Albert Hillary, a businessman who ran the Carson's chocolate factory in Glasgow. Hillary won the seat in 1922 by 764 votes and increased his majority to 1,917 at the next general election the following year. But Hillary's tenure in the Commons was brief. He lost his seat in 1924 by 2,315 votes to the Conservatives' Sir Frederick Rice, who was a building contractor by profession and had lost to Hillary the previous year. 1924 was the first time that Labour contested the Harwich seat, but the Labour candidate lost his deposit and Rice had over 50% of the vote anyway. Sir Frederick Rice stood down at the 1929 election and yet again the Conservatives failed to pass the Harwich seat on. This time the Liberal winner in Harwich was John Pybus CBE, an engineer and businessman who had become chairman of the engineering conglomerate English Electric in 1926. He defeated the Unionist candidate John Mayhew, who later served for 14 years as MP for East Ham North, by 2,700 votes; there was also an Independent Unionist candidate on the ballot, but he lost his deposit and Pybus had over 50% of the vote anyway. When the Ramsay Macdonald Labour government fell apart in the summer of 1931 and the first National Government was formed, John Pybus was offered and accepted the post of Minister of Transport in the new régime. He then joined the breakaway Liberal Nationals and was re-elected on their ticket in 1931 with a runaway win, polling 86.4% of the vote against only Labour opposition. Pybus remained in government until 1933, and he was created a baronet the following year. Sir John Pybus died on 23rd October 1935 at the age of 55, leaving no heir to his baronetcy. There was no by-election to replace him because the 1935 general election had been called a few days previously, and Parliament was dissolved two days after Pybus' death. The 1935 general election in Harwich marked a return to the Commons for National Liberal candidate Stanley Holmes, a chartered accountant and former London county councillor who had previously served as the Liberal MP for North East Derbyshire from 1918 to 1922. Holmes had lost that seat to Labour in 1922 by just five votes, a result which he challenged in the Election Court: but following a recount and adjudication by the Court on doubtful ballot papers, the Labour majority was increased to 15 votes. Harwich proved to be a much safer berth for Stanley Holmes, who won four terms of office here on the National Liberal ticket without serious opposition. He was knighted in 1945, and then left the Commons in 1954 by being elevated to the House of Lords as the first (and only) Lord Dovercourt. By this point the National Liberals were officially allied with the Conservatives, and for the resulting Harwich by-election of 11th February 1954 the defending candidate was Julian Ridsdale standing on a joint Conservative and Liberal ticket. This neatly reflected his upbringing as a nephew of a former Liberal MP and a former Conservative MP - the latter being none other than three-time Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin. During the Second World War Ridsdale had been a military intelligence officer specialising in Japan, which brought him into contact with a secretary called Paddy Bennett who married him in 1942; Bennett had been Iam Fleming's secretary, and the character of Miss Moneypenny in the James Bond universe is generally thought to be based on her. Julian Ridsdale had been a Conservative candidate in the 1951 general election, standing unsuccessfully against Labour in Paddington North. In the 1954 Harwich by-election his only opposition came from Labour's Shirley Catlin, who was 22 years old and standing in her first election: her previous main political role had been as chair of Oxford University Labour Club. Readers might know Catlin better under her married name of Shirley Williams. Ridsdale ended up winning by 19,532 votes to 13,535, a majority of 5,997 votes which represented almost no swing from the 1951 result. A rematch between Ridsdale and Catlin the following year saw the Conservative and Liberal majority increase to 9,464 votes. Ridsdale went on to win eleven terms of office as MP for Harwich, transitioning to become a Conservative MP after the National Liberals finally merged with the Tories in 1968. His frontbench career was brief, as a junior Air minister from 1962 to 1964, and his backbench position after that might well have been cemented by a full-throated defence of Enoch Powell's "Rivers of Blood" speech. Sir Julian, as he became in 1981, never lost his fascination with Japan: he chaired the British Japanese parliamentary group for 28 years and led a number of UK parliamentary delegations to the country. Major figures who took Julian Ridsdale on over the years, as well as Shirley Williams whom we have already mentioned, include William Robinson who was the Labour candidate here in 1959 and later briefly served as MP for Walthamstow East; David Winnick who was the Labour candidate here in 1964 and later had a long Parliamentary career in Croydon and Walsall; Liz Lynne who was the Liberal candidate here in 1987 and later served as a Lib Dem MP for Rochdale and MEP for the West Midlands; and Andrew Phillips who was the Labour candidate here in 1970 and later served as a Lib Dem member of the House of Lords and as the legal eagle on Jimmy Young's Radio 2 show. From 2003 to 2014 Phillips was the chancellor of the University of Essex, which was located within the Harwich constituency from its foundation in 1965 until boundary changes moved it into the Colchester North constituency in 1983. When he assumed that role the vice-chancellor of Essex was Sir Ivor Crewe, a former political academic at that university who co-directed the British Election Study for much of the 1970s and frequently appeared as an electoral commentator on TV and radio.
