Three by-elections on 13th August 2026, and we start with the Parliamentary Special:

Clacton

House of Commons, Essex; caused by the resignation of Reform UK MP Nigel Farage. The Clacton parliamentary seat was only created in 2010, but this area's position in the north-east corner of Essex has generally made the constituency map here stable over the decades and centuries. There is effectively only one predcessor seat covering the whole of the period since 1885, when single-member constituencies became the norm for UK parliamentary elections: but until 2010 that seat took its name from an older town which is no longer part of this constituency. The port of Harwich had been enfranchised in 1604, entitling it to send two members to Parliament who were elected by the borough corporation until the First Reform Act of 1832. In practice its elections were controlled by the government and particularly by the Treasury, which had a major influence in Harwich thanks to its control over lucrative jobs in the town's custome house and post office, and as a result Harwich has more than its fair share of notable MPs. The diarist Samuel Pepys represented the town briefly in 1679 and again from 1685 to 1689, while he was secretary of the Admiralty. From 1807 to 1826 one of Harwich's two seats was held successively by Tories William Huskisson, a long-serving and high-profile statesman who is now best remembered for being killed by a train on the opening day of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway in 1830; then by Nicholas Vansittart, whose service as Chancellor of the Exchequer from 1812 to 1823 has since been beaten only by Gladstone (who had four separate stints in the job); and then by the foreign secretary George Canning, who was subsequently prime minister for a period of approximately 2.4 Liz Trusses. A series of elections here in the late 1840s and early 1850s were voided for bribery and corruption, and after two failed Harwich by-elections in 1851 Parliament refused to issue a writ for a third by-election for almost a year, effectively leaving one of the town's seats vacant as punishment for bad behaviour. Having been reduced to one seat by the Second Reform in 1868, Harwich was disenfranchised by the Third Reform in 1885 but its name was transferred to one the new county division covering the north-east corner of Essex. The 1885 Harwich constituency contained the whole of what is now Tendring district plus the rural area surrounding Colchester, which was the only one of the three ancient Essex parliamentary boroughs left standing by the redistribution. Nonetheless Essex came out of the 1885 boundary changes as a winner, with the creation of two new county seats and the enfranchisement of West Ham ensuring that the county went up from 10 MPs to 11. The reorganised Harwich seat was won at its first contest in 1885 by Conservative James Round, who came from a prominent local landowning family and had represented the predecessor seat of East Essex since 1868. Round was one of the select group of MPs who had played first-class cricket, and he turned out in a total of 22 first-class matches for Oxford and MCC in the 1860s; he also played for Essex in the days before Essex was a first-class county. James Round's first-class career batting average was 16.85, but he had better success at the ballot box where he never lost an election; his closest call came in the 1892 general election where his majority over the Liberals was 305. Nobody opposed Round's final re-election in 1900, and he was sworn of the Privy Council in 1902 as a reward for his long service in the House of Commons. James Round retired at the 1906 general election after 40 years on the green benches. His Harwich seat then fell in the Liberal landslide to Arthur Lever, a former Royal Fusiliers officer who had retired from the Army with the rank of major. Lever's majority over the Conservative candidate Harry Newton was 342 votes. This narrow margin wasn't sustainable when the Liberal landslide subsided. A rematch between Lever and Newton in January 1910 resulted in a win for Newton by over 1,000 votes, and Harry Newton went on to serve as MP for Harwich for 12 years. Newton had been called to the Bar, but his father Sir Alfred Newton, 1st Baronet, was a Hull-born businessman who had become a City financier and chairman of the board of Harrods. Newton senior was also involved in City politics and he rose to become Lord Mayor of London in 1899-1900, the year that the Boer War broke out; his contribution to this was to establish the City of London Imperial Volunteers to fight in South Africa, and young Harry went with them. In 1921 Harry Newton succeeded to his father's titles and became Sir Harry Newton, 2nd Baronet, in controversial circumstances. Sir Alfred Newton had suddenly died of a seizure at Harrods, and the police investigation found that he had taken prescribed indigestion medicine from a bottle which contained enough strychnine to kill several people. The medicine bottle had been made up by the Harrods pharmacist. After the inquest jury had heard evidence from the pharmacist about her working practices and seen a post-mortem report which indicated that Alfred Newton's heart was in poor condition, the coroner recorded a verdict of death by heart failure. Sir Harry Newton retired as MP for Harwich in 1922, and again the Conservatives failed to pass the seat on to their new candidate. The Harwich constituency had been redrawn in 1918 with the same boundaries as the modern Tendring district, and it returned the Liberal Party candidate Albert Hillary, a businessman who ran the Carson's chocolate factory in Glasgow. Hillary won the seat in 1922 by 764 votes and increased his majority to 1,917 at the next general election the following year. But Hillary's tenure in the Commons was brief. He lost his seat in 1924 by 2,315 votes to the Conservatives' Sir Frederick Rice, who was a building contractor by profession and had lost to Hillary the previous year. 1924 was the first time that Labour contested the Harwich seat, but the Labour candidate lost his deposit and Rice had over 50% of the vote anyway. Sir Frederick Rice stood down at the 1929 election and yet again the Conservatives failed to pass the Harwich seat on. This time the Liberal winner in Harwich was John Pybus CBE, an engineer and businessman who had become chairman of the engineering conglomerate English Electric in 1926. He defeated the Unionist candidate John Mayhew, who later served for 14 years as MP for East Ham North, by 2,700 votes; there was also an Independent Unionist candidate on the ballot, but he lost his deposit and Pybus had over 50% of the vote anyway. When the Ramsay Macdonald Labour government fell apart in the summer of 1931 and the first National Government was formed, John Pybus was offered and accepted the post of Minister of Transport in the new régime. He then joined the breakaway Liberal Nationals and was re-elected on their ticket in 1931 with a runaway win, polling 86.4% of the vote against only Labour opposition. Pybus remained in government until 1933, and he was created a baronet the following year. Sir John Pybus died on 23rd October 1935 at the age of 55, leaving no heir to his baronetcy. There was no by-election to replace him because the 1935 general election had been called a few days previously, and Parliament was dissolved two days after Pybus' death. The 1935 general election in Harwich marked a return to the Commons for National Liberal candidate Stanley Holmes, a chartered accountant and former London county councillor who had previously served as the Liberal MP for North East Derbyshire from 1918 to 1922. Holmes had lost that seat to Labour in 1922 by just five votes, a result which he challenged in the Election Court: but following a recount and adjudication by the Court on doubtful ballot papers, the Labour majority was increased to 15 votes. Harwich proved to be a much safer berth for Stanley Holmes, who won four terms of office here on the National Liberal ticket without serious opposition. He was knighted in 1945, and then left the Commons in 1954 by being elevated to the House of Lords as the first (and only) Lord Dovercourt. By this point the National Liberals were officially allied with the Conservatives, and for the resulting Harwich by-election of 11th February 1954 the defending candidate was Julian Ridsdale standing on a joint Conservative and Liberal ticket. This neatly reflected his upbringing as a nephew of a former Liberal MP and a former Conservative MP - the latter being none other than three-time Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin. During the Second World War Ridsdale had been a military intelligence officer specialising in Japan, which brought him into contact with a secretary called Paddy Bennett who married him in 1942; Bennett had been Iam Fleming's secretary, and the character of Miss Moneypenny in the James Bond universe is generally thought to be based on her. Julian Ridsdale had been a Conservative candidate in the 1951 general election, standing unsuccessfully against Labour in Paddington North. In the 1954 Harwich by-election his only opposition came from Labour's Shirley Catlin, who was 23 years old and standing in her first election: her previous main political role had been as chair of Oxford University Labour Club. Readers might know Catlin better under her married name of Shirley Williams. Ridsdale ended up winning by 19,532 votes to 13,535, a majority of 5,997 votes which represented almost no swing from the 1951 result. A rematch between Ridsdale and Catlin at the following year's general election saw the Conservative and Liberal majority increase to 9,464 votes. Ridsdale went on to win eleven terms of office as MP for Harwich, transitioning to become a Conservative MP after the National Liberals finally merged with the Tories in 1968. His frontbench career was brief, as a junior Air minister from 1962 to 1964, and his backbench position after that might well have been cemented by a full-throated defence of Enoch Powell's "Rivers of Blood" speech. Sir Julian, as he became in 1981, never lost his fascination with Japan: he chaired the British Japanese parliamentary group for 28 years and led a number of UK parliamentary delegations to the country. Major figures who took Julian Ridsdale on over the years, as well as Shirley Williams whom we have already mentioned, include William Robinson who was the Labour candidate here in 1959 and later briefly served as MP for Walthamstow East; David Winnick who was the Labour candidate here in 1964 and later had a long Parliamentary career in Croydon and Walsall; Liz Lynne who was the Liberal candidate here in 1987 and later served as a Lib Dem MP for Rochdale and MEP for the West Midlands; and Andrew Phillips who was the Labour candidate here in 1970 and later served as a Lib Dem member of the House of Lords and as the legal eagle on Jimmy Young's Radio 2 show. From 2003 to 2014 Phillips was the chancellor of the University of Essex, which was located within the Harwich constituency from its foundation in 1965 until boundary changes transferred it into the Colchester North constituency in 1983. When he assumed that role the vice-chancellor of Essex was Sir Ivor Crewe, a former political academic at that university who co-directed the British Election Study for much of the 1970s and frequently appeared as an electoral commentator on TV and radio. I hope Sir Ivor won't mind me reproducing the Harwich entry from the 1984 book British Parliamentary Constituencies: A Statistical Compendium which he co-wrote with Anthony Fox, and which I have found invaluable over the years. In case you were wondering, the number 21 in the top-right-hand corner is a demographic code for "resort and retirement areas", with Harwich being one of 29 constituencies so classed according to the 1981 census. Many readers of this column will remember Professor King as a BBC election night analyst, a role he performed at every general election from 1983 to 2005. Sir Julian Ridsdale finally called it a day at the 1992 general election after 38 years' service, and this time the Harwich Conservatives finally managed to pass a seat on to their new candidate after their MP retired. This election marked a return to the Commons for the accident-prone Iain Sproat, who had previously been MP for Aberdeen South from 1970 to 1983. Following boundary changes to that seat, Sproat had attempted to do the chicken run to Roxburgh and Berwickshire - but not only did he lose there, but his Conservative successor held on to Aberdeen South. With a much safer majority in his new English constituency - he had defeated the Lib Dem candidate by over 17,000 votes in 1992 - a frontbench career beckoned, and Iain Sproat spent most of the 1993-97 Parliament as minister for sport in the Major government. So it must have been a shock to Iain Sproat when he went down to defeat in the 1997 Blair landslide, as Harwich returned its first and only Labour MP with a majority of 1,216 votes. It was certainly a shock to the Labour candidate Ivan Henderson, a trade union official at the port of Harwich, who had to phone his boss on the morning after the 1997 election and say that he wouldn't be able to work the shift he was down for that day because he'd won the election. A rematch between Henderson and Sproat in 2001 saw Henderson increase his majority to 2,596 votes. Hendeson lost his seat to the Conservatives in 2005 but he is still active in local politics: he served as mayor of Harwich in 2021-22, he was the Essex county councillor for Harwich from 2013 to May 2026 and led the county council's Labour group for most of that period, and he has been a Tendring councillor since 2011 where he currently represents Harwich and Kingsway ward. One possibly-decisive factor in Henderson's 1997 win was that Harwich had the best score in any constituency that year for the short-lived Referendum Party, whose candidate Jeffrey Titford - a former Conservative councillor in Clacton - polled 9.2% of the vote. It was a sign of things to come. Titford later served as leader of UKIP twice (once on an active basis) and he was an MEP for the East of England from 1999 to 2019. Jeffrey Titford was the UKIP candidate for Harwich in 2005, but on that occasion he lost his deposit as the Conservatives took the Harwich constituency back by 920 votes. The new MP was Douglas Carswell, who was born in Africa to Scottish parents who were both doctors: his father Wilson Carswell had diagnosed Uganda's first AIDS cases in the 1980s. After graduating in history from UEA and King's College London, Carswell had worked in TV broadcasting in Italy before joining the Conservative Party's policy unit. He had stood in the 2001 general election as the Conservative candidate against Tony Blair in Sedgefield. In Parliament, Carswell emerged as a campaigner on traditional Conservative values, calling for radical political reform (he was the only Conservative MP to support proportional representation) and withdrawal from the European Union. He was a big advocate of a recall process for MPs. Douglas Carswell's position was significantly improved by boundary changes for the 2010 general election, which took the town of Harwich - and with it Ivan Henderson's powerbase - out of the Harwich constituency. This meant a new name for the seat was needed, and "Clacton" was chosen after the main town in the area. The new Clacton seat was projected to be safely Conservative in 2005 conditions, and a rematch between Carswell and Henderson in 2010 saw Carswell increase his majority to 12,068 votes. In 2014 Douglas Carswell defected to UKIP. Unlike most defectors in Parliament or at lower levels of government, he immediately resigned his seat in order to seek re-election to the House of Commons under his new purple colours - something which he achieved with ease at the resulting first Clacton by-election of 9th October 2014. Carswell even increased his majority to 12,404 votes, defeating the Conservative candidate Giles Watling by 60-25 in percentage terms. Round 2 of Carswell v Watling at the 2015 general election was closer, with Carswell winning his fourth term of office by 3,437 votes. Douglas Carswell quit UKIP to become an independent MP in March 2017 - not triggering a by-election this time - and he didn't seek re-election at the 2017 general election a few months later. The new UKIP candidate Paul Oakley came nowhere near holding the seat - he finished third with just 7.6% of the vote - and the Conservatives' Giles Watling cruised to victory by 15,828 votes over Labour. Watling was a Conservative councillor for Frinton ward from 2007 to 2019, but before entering the Commons he had been an actor and was best known for playing Oswald the vicar in the TV series Bread. Unfortunately, his last credited film role was a cameo in the notorious 2012 film Run for Your Wife, which starred Danny Dyer as a bigamist and Denise van Outen and Sarah Harding as his wives. Run for Your Wife had run for nine years in the 1980s and 1990s as a successful stage farce in the West End but it completely failed to make the transition to the silver screen, with the film having a rare 0% rating on Rotten Tomatoes and appearing on Wikipedia's "List of 21st-century films considered the worst". This list is one of my guilty pleasures because the savage reviews on that page are always good for a laugh, and Run for Your Wife's entry does not disappoint with critics' quotes including "makes The Dick Emery Show look edgy and contemporary" (Peter Bradshaw in the Grauniad) and "never in the field of light entertainment have so many actors sacrificed so much dignity in the cause of so few jokes" (Anthony Quinn in the Independent). The Metro's review of Run for Your Wife opined that "no-one emerges unscathed among the cameo-packed cast that reads largely like a roll-call for Brit TV legends you'd previously suspected deceased", which is a bit unfair on Giles Watling. Not that any of this had an effect on his support at the ballot box: Watling was re-elected in 2019 nearly 25,000 votes ahead of Labour candidate Kevin Bonavia, who has since made it into the Commons as MP for Stevenage. Despite that lead, Giles Watling went down to defeat at the 2024 general election when he was opposed by the Reform UK leader Nigel Farage, who needs no introduction. Farage had been an MEP for south-east England from 1999 to 2020, but this was the first time he had got into the Commons in eight attempts. He won Clacton in July 2024 with 46% of the vote against 28% for the Conservatives' Giles Watling and 16% for Labour's Jovan Owosu-Nepaul; Farage's numerical majority over Watling was 8,405 votes. Nigel Farage is never short of controversy, and in recent months some attention has been focused on his financial interests. This is all a bit murky at the moment, with allegations swirling around in particular over a gift of £5 million received from a cryptocurrency millionaire based in Thailand which wasn't properly declared to the Parliamentary authorities, and over Farage's professional relationship with a convicted fraudster whose mother has donated £500,000 to Reform UK. The Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards is looking into all this, so hopefully things will get a bit clearer in due course. Farage has denied any wrongdoing by the unusually forceful method of seeking re-election as a vote of confidence from his Clacton constituents. He tendered his resignation from the Commons last month by applying for the post of Steward and Bailiff of the Manor of Northstead. He is the defending Reform UK candidate for this by-election. Reform UK offered to pay for the cost of the by-election themselves, but this is strictly against the rules (and one wonders where the money for that would have come from). Back in the days of rotten boroughs candidates were often expected to pay the cost of polling themselves, but this meant that elections were out of reach for all but the very wealthy - and that elections were often uncontested. Today candidates for Parliament are expected to pay a £500 deposit in order to stand, which they will get back if they exceed 5% of the votes cost (we'll come back to this in a moment), but this sum deliberately doesn't cover the cost of organising and staffing an election. That cost is borne by the returning officer in the first instance and ultimately by the Treasury, a policy which has been the case for an extremely long time because reduces the entry barriers for candidates and opens up politics to the masses. And the masses have certainly taken to this by-election, as one look at the candidate list reveals. None of the established political parties other than the defending Reform UK have shown any interest at all in contesting this by-election, but the days when parliamentary elections could be uncontested - as most recently happened in Harwich in 1900 - are long over. Instead we have the ridiculous sight of Nigel Farage fighting 33 relative unknowns in the largest field ever assembled for a parliamentary election. The sheer number of candidates has exposed some problems with the parliamentary elections rules. One is a potential issue with the deposit rule. To quote rule 53(4) of the parliamentary rules, "the deposit shall be forfeited if a poll is taken and, after the counting of the votes by the returning officer (including any recount) is completed, the candidate is found not to have polled more than one twentieth of the total number of votes polled by all the candidates". A little bit of maths will reveal that the maximum number of candidates who can get their deposit back under this rule is nineteen: if you have 19 candidates in an election who each poll one vote then they are all over 1/20th of the vote, but if you have 20 candidates in an election who each poll one vote then none of them are over 1/20th of the vote. Since we have 34 candidates here, it follows that at least 15 of them (and probably more) will lose their £500 deposits and the Treasury can bank £7,500 straight away. This is fair enough, but with 34 candidates we also run into the issue that somebody could win this thing and still lose their deposit. Consider an election with 34 candidates in which 34,001 votes are cast, candidate A wins with 1,001 votes and everybody else scores 1,000. The winning score here is 1,001/34,001 or 2.94%, so despite winning candidate A still doesn't get their deposit back. This possibility is admittedly remote but it does seem a little unfair. The parliamentary elections rules also contain an Appendix of Forms, which among other things gives specifications for the ballot paper. These are implicitly written on the assumption that all the candidates are to be arranged in one column. This is the template given for the front of the ballot paper in the current edition of the rules: This is followed by a set of "Directions as to printing the ballot paper", which are to be observed "so far as is practicable". One of the directions states that "The horizontal rules [separating the particulars of the candidates from one another] must be equally spaced and the space between each of them must be a minimum of 2.5 centimetres". With 34 candidates, that means that the ballot paper for this election is 85 centimetres long plus space at the top for the instructions, for a total of around 90 centimetres. Or, as the older voters of Clacton might put it, about three feet. The last time the record for candidates in a parliamentary by-election was broken was in Haltemprice and Howden in 2008, with 26 candidates standing then. That should have resulted in a ballot paper around 70 centimetres long, but the returning officer for Haltemprice and Howden couldn't find a printing firm which could handle that. The election staff then had to seek emergency permission from the Electoral Commission to redesign the ballot paper with the 26 candidates arranged in two columns. This time the Clacton returning officer has found a printer who can handle sheets of paper which are 90 centimetres long, but at a price: the printing costs for this by-election are far higher than normal. There are also going to be all sorts of issues at the count: apart from the difficulties in physically handling such a volume of paper, the ballot is simply too long to fit on Tendring's normal counting tables. Tables are having to be doubled up to counter this, which will reduce the space available in the hall and lead to a slower count. One opinion poll for this circus has been attempted by the polling company Survation, which gave Farage 73% of the vote against 20% for his main challenger, Count Binface. Binface, who leads his own eponymous party, claims to be the leader of the Recyclons from the planet Sigma IX. His earthly alter-ego is Jonathan Harvey, a comedy scriptwriter who has worked on Have I Got News for You and The Thick of It, among other problems. Binface's terrestrial address on the ballot paper is given as in the Sussex Weald constituency. The character of Count Binface arose from a copyright dispute. Harvey had stood for election in 2017 against the then Prime Minister Theresa May in Maidenhead under the character of "Lord Buckethead", which had originated in the 1984 science fiction film Gremloids. Released in the USA under the title Hyperspace, Gremloids was a Star Wars parody in which, according to Rotten Tomatoes (which has no rating for it), "Lord Buckethead (Robert Bloodworth) and his evil minions mistakenly land in a backwoods Southern town and proceed with their search for a rebel princess". The film's UK video distributor Mike Lee decided to publicise Gremloids by standing in the 1987 general election against Margaret Thatcher, under the name of Lord Buckethead who was supposedly representing the Gremloids Party (this was before the days when poltiical parties were required to be registered). Lee repeated the trick in 1992, this time standing against John Major. Unfortunately, Harvey's 2017 campaign and other subsequent media appearances came to the attention of the director of Gremloids Todd Durham, who then wrote to Harvey asserting his legal rights over the character of Lord Buckethead. Durham took the character of Buckethead back and enlisted film journalist David Hughes to stand in the 2019 general election under that name, while Harvey's response was to create Count Binface as a blatant ripoff of what was already a blatant ripoff of Darth Vader. Binface and Buckethead went head-to-head in the 2019 general election in Uxbridge and South Ruislip, with Buckethead - now on the ticket of the Monster Raving Loony Party - polling 125 votes to 69 for Binface. Nothing more has been heard of Lord Buckethead since, but Binface has had two high-profile runs in the 2021 and 2024 London Mayor election, polling over 24,000 votes and around 1% on both occasions and finishing in the top half of the field in 2021. He was last seen on a ballot paper at the Makerfield by-election two months ago, where he polled 95 votes and finished in the top half of the field: 7th place out of 14 candidates. Andy Burnham looked pleased to be standing next to Binface at the declaration. The grandfathers of silly election campaigns are of course to be found in the Official Monster Raving Loony Party (beware of imitations), who have pulled off their own stunt by nominating three candidates for the one seat available in this by-election. This is something they have done before at local council level: there were nine Loony candidates for one ward in the 2012 election to Elmbridge council, and a Kingston upon Thames council by-election in May 2021 had 13 Loony candidates - six of whom managed to beat the Trade Unionist and Socialist Coalition's nominee. Both of those efforts were orchestrated by Peter "Chinners" Chinnery, who deserved some sort of award for this: in this column five years ago I suggested that the Turner Prize would probably be the most appropriate award, but sadly their judges didn't agree. The main barrier to pulling this stunt at parliamentary elections is that parliamentary elections have a £500 deposit, so standing multiple candidates for a single vacancy gets very expensive very quickly as well as being a somewhat dubious electoral tactic.
