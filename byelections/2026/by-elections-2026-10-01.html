"All the right votes, but not necessarily in the right order"

It's the start of a new month, and Andrew's Previews has six by-elections to bring you on 1st October 2026. The Conservatives and Reform UK are defending two seats each, Labour one and the remaining seat was previously held by an independent councillor. Two of today's polls are north of the Border - in the case of our first by-election, very far north of the Border indeed...

Wick and East Caithness

Highland council, Scotland; caused by the resignation of independent councillor Willie Mackay.

The name of John o' Groats is synonymous with the end of the road - or, depending on your viewpoint, the start of the road. For many years this tiny village on Scotland's north coast was the northern terminus of the A9 trunk road from Edinburgh (since renumbered A99, after the A9 was diverted to Thurso), and the only further way from here was by sea. In the fifteenth century a Dutchman called Jan de Groot - from whom the village takes its name - ran a ferry service from here to Orkney, and the modern ferry - which is for foot passengers only - takes 40 minutes to cross the Pentland Firth to Burwick on South Ronaldsay. The embarkation point for vehicle traffic is nearby Gills Bay, from where crossings take 1 hour to St Margaret's Hope on South Ronaldsay. Both ferry routes pass the uninhabited island of Stroma, which is the northernmost part of the Highland council area.

John o' Groats does not mark the Scottish mainland's north-east corner: that is a couple of miles to the east at Duncansby Head, where a lighthouse has warned of the danger to shipping for over two centuries. To the south of here two prominent sea stacks stand off the east coast. Tourists who have exhausted the delights of John o' Groats might wish to visit the Castle of Mey, which was extensively restored in the 1950s as a holiday home for Queen Elizabeth Bowes-Lyon and is now open to the public in the summer (as long as her grandson Charles III isn't in residence).

This north-eastern corner of Scotland is covered by the county of Caithness, which has a total population of around 25,000. Most of this is accounted for by the burghs of Thurso and Wick, leaving the rest of the county virtually empty. The largest polling station here outside Wick is in Lybster, which lies on the south coast of Caithness and has 789 electors: this was a major fishing port in the 19th century, with only Wick and Fraserburgh having more herring fishermen. The polling station for John o' Groats and the other villages on the north coast, which is located in the village of Canisbay, has 485 electors on the roll. And this is the relatively flat and fertile part of the county: to the south of Thurso is the Flow Country, a vast area of blanket bog and peatland which was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2024.

Within the Flow Country and on the boundary of this ward is the extremely isolated Altnabreac railway station on the Far North line, which is located around 10 miles from the nearest road. Altnabreac recorded a grand total of zero passengers in 2024-25, because the station was closed that year due to an access dispute between the railway and a local resident. The station is now open again, but the dispute does not appear to have been resolved.

The railway terminates at Wick, which lies at the mouth of the Wick River and was historically dependent on the fishing industry which led to major population growth in the 19th century. Much of this was concentrated in the suburb of Pulteneytown, which was named after a governor of the British Fisheries Society and was a separate town until 1902. This was the original location of the Caithness Glass factory, which supplied the glass bowls awarded to the winners of BBC Mastermind. The factory closed in 2006, which was the year that Guinness World Records recognised Wick's Ebenezer Place as the world's shortest street, with a length of 2.06 metres.

Wick and East Caithness ward was created in 2017 as a result of boundary changes, which had reduced the size of Highland council from 80 councillors to 74. Caithness was the big loser in this redistribution, going down from 10 councillors to 8. Previously Wick and Thurso had been wards of their own with a Landward Caithness ward covering the rest of the county.

The previous Landward Caithness ward was dominated by independent candidates, and going into the 2017 election it had a full slate of four independent councillors following a by-election gain from the SNP in November 2013: the SNP councillor had been forced to resign after it came out that he had gone over the expense limit in the 2012 election. Wick ward started off with independent dominance too, but an SNP gain in an April 2011 by-election broke the mould; the new SNP councillor, Gail Ross, was re-elected in 2012 at the top of the poll with 46% of the vote.

Ross didn't seek re-election in 2017, and the first election for the new Wick and East Caithness ward returned to independent dominance, with five independent candidates polling 62% of the vote between them. Top of the poll was Willie Mackay, outgoing councillor for Landward Caithness ward, who polled 22% of the vote and was elected on the first count. Also elected on the first count was new independent candidate Nicola Sinclair, who polled 21%. Another Sinclair on the ballot was Andrew Sinclair of the Conservatives, who polled 14% and won the third seat. Raymond Bremner of the SNP (12%) narrowly defeated Neil Macdonald (9%), the outgoing Labour councillor for Wick ward, for the fourth and final seat.

Nicola Sinclair resigned from the council in 2021 to go back to her previous career in local journalism, and the resulting August 2021 by-election was won by Jill Tilt of the Liberal Democrats, who narrowly defeated independent candidate Bill Fernie by 986 votes to 963 after transfers. The first preferences had been 27% for the Lib Dems, 26% for Fernie, 25% for the SNP and 22% for the Conservatives.

The Lib Dems then consolidated their gain at the 2022 election, with their new candidate Jan McEwan topping the poll on 30% of the vote. She was elected on the first count alongside the SNP's Raymond Bremner, who was re-elected with 25% and currently leads the council's SNP group; and the other two seats went to new Conservative Andrew Jarvie (15%) and re-elected independent candidate Willie Mackay (13%). Bill Fernie again finished as runner-up, with Jarvie winning the final seat by 86 votes after transfers. If we rerun the count for a single vacancy, then the Lib Dems' Jan McEwan would have beaten the SNP's Raymond Bremner after transfers by the lopsided margin of 61-39.

The Liberal Democrats now represent Caithness in both Parliaments. Jamie Stone, the chair of the Commons petitions committee, has been the Westminster MP for Caithness, Sutherland and Easter Ross since 2017; while in May the Holyrood seat of Caithness, Sutherland and Ross was gained by the Lib Dem's David Green. He unseated the previous SNP constituency MSP, junior minister Maree Todd, but Todd was nonetheless re-elected to Holyrood as a regional MSP for the Highlands and Islands.

So the Lib Dems might well fancy their chances in this by-election. The voters of Wick and East Caithness ward are now being called out for their second by-election in five years to replace long-serving independent councillor Willie Mackay, who was first elected for Landward Caithness ward in 2007 and transferred here following boundary changes in 2017. Mackay's council career ended in disgrace in July, when he resigned from the council - prompting the 13th by-election of this Highland council term - after voting in favour of allowing one David Brown, who is currently serving a prison sentence of 6 years and 9 months for raping a female passenger in his taxi, to keep his taxi operator's licence. This was passed by the Highland Council's licensing committee by six votes to four, resulting in a political storm and the resignation of three other councillors from the committee.

One new independent candidate has come forward to succeed Mackay: Dave Buchanan is a chef and businessman who gives an address in Wick and is very unhappy with the current Highland council leadership. The Lib Dems have selected first-time candidate Julie Calder. Standing for the SNP is John Glen, who is a former depute head at Wick high school. The Conservative candidate is Charlotte Gibson, who contested North, West and Central Sutherland ward in 2022. And completing this ballot is Reform UK's Iain Richmond, who stood in a by-election for faraway Inverness Central ward two years ago.

Forth and Endrick

Stirling council, Scotland; caused by the death of Conservative councillor Paul Henke.

Our second Scottish by-election covers a large rural area which is not that far to the north of Glasgow, but which is partly in a National Park. Forth and Endrick ward takes in the bonny, bonny eastern bank of Loch Lomond and the summit of Ben Lomond, which are located within the Loch Lomond and the Trossachs National Park. With an altitude of 974 metres or 3,196 feet, Ben Lomond is Scotland's most southerly Munro. Its summit has fine views of the Highlands and Lowlands.

However, the focus of Forth and Endrick ward is the villages between Loch Lomond to the west, Stirling to the east and Milngavie to the south. The eastward-flowing River Forth forms the ward's northern boundary, while the westward-flowing Endrick Water drains into Loch Lomond. Within the Endrick valley we find Balfron, one of three villages in this ward with slightly more than 2,000 electors on the roll: this was the birthplace of the noted Glasgow architect Alexander "Greek" Thomson.

To the south of here is Killearn, which lies at the junction of the Endrick Water and the Blane Water which marks the western end of the Campsie Fells. There is a large obelisk here commemorating George Buchanan, a Renaissance scholar and humanist who was born in Killearn in 1506. The road to Glasgow now proceeds south along the valley of Strath Blane, in which is located the Glengoyne whisky distillery and the twin villages of Strathblane and Blanefield. At the southern end of the ward, less than two miles from Milngavie and the start of Glasgow's urban area, are the ruins of Mugdock Castle which has been here since the 14th century and was historically the stronghold of the Clan Graham; its grounds now form Mugdock Country Park.

All this was historically part of Stirlingshire, and Stirling still remains the local seat of government. Following boundary changes for the 2017 Forth and Endrick ward returns three Stirling councillors, with the 2017 election returning two Conservatives and an SNP councillor, Graham Lambie, who defeated his running-mate Ian Muirhead for the final seat by 13 votes. Lambie suddenly died in February 2021 aged 62, and the Conservatives gained the resulting by-election on the undercard of the 2021 Scottish Parliament election.

The Conservatives did not defend their by-election gain at the 2022 election which saw a clearout of the ward's councillors as the three available seats split three ways. First preferences were 36% for the Conservatives, 35% for the SNP and 14% for Labour, who got transfers from outgoing Conservative councillor Rob Davies standing as an independent and then from the SNP surplus. Paul Henke won the final seat for the Conservatives, defeating his running-mate Thomas Heald by 116 votes. If we rerun the count for a single seat then Henke would have beaten the ward's SNP councillor Rosemary Fraser after transfers by 2,534 votes to 2,525, a majority of just nine votes.

Overall the 2022 Stirling council election returned 8 SNP councillors, 7 Conservatives, 6 Labour, a Green and an independent. Initially Labour formed a rather precarious minority administration, but by-election losses to the SNP eventually made that untenable. Stirling council is presently under SNP leadership, with a cabinet which includes the Green councillor.

One of the Labour by-election losses was a failure to defend the seat in Stirling East ward held by Chris Kane, who was elected in 2024 as the Labour MP for Stirling and Strathallan. Kane defeated the previous SNP MP and MEP Alyn Smith, who returned to elected politics in May in his third Parliament when he was elected as the SNP constituency MSP for Stirling. Smith is now a junior Scottish Government minister, with responsibility for innovation, technology and tertiary education. Second place in Stirling four months ago went to the Conservatives' Stephen Kerr, who was nonetheless re-elected to Holyrood as a regional MSP for Mid Scotland and Fife.

The voters of Forth and Endrick ward are now being called out for their second by-election in five years, and for the seventh by-election of this Stirling council term, to replace the Conservative councillor Paul Henke who died in July at the age of 76. Henke had a varied life: he was born in South Wales into a Polish immigrant family, and his first career was as a Royal Navy officer specialising in demining operations. This led to further work running a deep sea diving operation off Nigeria, after which Henke moved into financial services, then thriller writing and eventually elected politics.

Henke became the Scottish chairman of the UK Independence Party, and he contested this ward as the UKIP candidate in 2007 and 2012. He then fell out with the party over the selection of David Coburn as the lead UKIP candidate for the 2014 European elections in Scotland. The national UKIP leadership were not impressed, and they suspended Henke from the party for 100 years. But due process had not been followed (well, what did you expect from a 100-year suspension?), and Paul Henke got his party membership back following legal action in the Central London county court. He then joined the Conservatives and stood for them in Bannockburn ward in 2017, before finally making it to elected in office in 2022 for Forth and Endrick ward.

Defending this seat for the Conservatives is Katie Harrison, a management consultant from Balfron who is a trustee of the National Youth Orchestra of Scotland. The SNP have selected Mhairi Duncan, another consultant who has a background in the tourism industry: she is based within the National Park in Drymen, which is one of the smaller villages in this ward. Labour have a candidate with previous local government experience: Paul Godzik was an Edinburgh councillor from 2007 to 2017, representing Meadows/Morningside ward, and in May's Holyrood election he was the Labour candidate for Falkirk West. Robert Davies, who was a Conservative councillor for this ward from 2017 to 2022 when he lost re-election as an independent candidate, is back for another go; the Lib Dems have reselected their regular candidate here James Maclaren; Andrew Adam is the Scottish Green nominee; and Reform UK have selected Jeremy McDonald who was previously (2017-22) a Conservative councillor for the neighbouring Trossachs and Teith ward.

Amble; and
Hirst

Northumberland council; caused respectively by the death of Arty Hume and the disqualification of John Allen. Both were Reform UK councillors.

Our four English by-elections today all share a feature which up to now has been unusual, but which might get more common as time goes on. They all have a candidate from Restore Britain, the far-right group associated with the ex-Reform UK Great Yarmouth MP Rupert Lowe. Before today Restore Britain had only contested three local by-elections (the Rupert Lowe-supported candidates who won seats in Great Yarmouth in May were elected under the banner of Great Yarmouth First, not Restore Britain).

Restore Britain's local début came in July with a score of 3.5% and 6th out of 7 in the Old Catton division of Norfolk county council; in August Restore Britain have polled 6.5% in the Barnsley ward of Penistone East (4th out of 8) and 10.7% in the Dover council ward of Guston, Kingsdown and St Margaret's-at-Cliffe (5th out of 6). The first two of those by-elections saw Reform UK lose council seats that they were defending, while in the Guston case Restore Britain's 284 votes were clearly decisive in the final result: the split on the radical right allowed the Green Party candidate to come through the middle and defeat Reform UK by 767 votes to 733, a margin of 34 votes. Lowe appears to see these results as a promising start, and we can see from the candidate lists for October that Restore Britain is now getting its act together sufficiently to regularly nominate candidates in local by-elections.

Two of today's English by-elections are Reform UK defences, both of which are for seats on Northumberland council. Amble by the Sea is a small port on the North Sea coast, located at the mouth of the River Coquet, which was dependent for its prosperity on fishing, the coal industry and the RAF Acklington airbase; but the last two of these are long gone, leaving fishing and tourism. The beaches here are wide and sandy, while only ¾ mile off the coast is Coquet Island which is managed by the RSPB as a nature reserve. Coquet Island has tens of thousands of puffins nesting there, together with the UK's largest population of the rare roseate tern. The area's human population has been very little affected by immigration: in the 2021 census Amble ward, which was larger then, made the top 50 wards in England and Wales for White British ethnicity (97.8%) and the top 90 for residents born in the UK (97.9%).

Hirst ward is rather different in character but shares a coalmining history. This is one of 6½ Northumberland wards covering the town of Ashington, which was traditionally divided into two by the railway line: Hirst was the east of the town. Hirst Park lies at the southern end of this ward, surrounded by miners' terraced houses which still make up the majority of this ward's housing stock: in the 2021 census Hirst ward, which then had slightly different boundaries, made the top 40 wards in England and Wales and had the second-highest figure in the North East for terraced housing. The ward also had the highest figure in the North East, and was in the top 40 nationally, for residents with 5+ GCSE passes or equivalent. The local economy is now underpinned by the healthcare sector, and Wansbeck General Hospital is just outside the ward boundary.

The northern half of Hirst ward is the open space of Queen Elizabeth II Country Park, which includes the surviving pithead buildings of Woodhorn Colliery. Next to these is a new museum building, opened in 2006, which houses the Northumberland county archives and has a permanent exhibition showing the work of the Ashington Group. This consisted of pitmen with a passion for art, who met weekly in Ashington from 1934 to 1983 and painted scenes from their own lives.

Ashington's connection with the coal industry is such that the town's Labour MP since 2010 has been Ian Lavery, who had succeeded Arthur Scargill as president of the NUM in 2002. Lavery originally represented the Wansbeck constituency, which was redrawn in 2024 with the name of Blyth and Ashington. Amble has a very different parliamentary history as a long-standing part of the sprawling Berwick-upon-Tweed constituency, which was expanded in 2024 and given the new name of North Northumberland: its first-ever Labour MP, elected in 2024, is David Smith who defeated the outgoing Berwick MP Anne-Marie Trevelyan.

Reform UK narrowly polled the most votes in the 2025 election to Northumberland council - 29.3%, against 28.8% for the Conservatives and 22% for Labour - but it's seats that count, and the Conservatives ended up with a 26-23 lead over Reform UK on Northumberland council. The remaining seats were split 8 to Labour, 7 to independents, 3 to the Lib Dems and 2 to the Greens. The Conservatives continue to run the show here as a minority administration.

The Northumberland branch of Reform UK have already lost one by-election to the Conservatives this year, in Cramlington South West in April, and they have two more seats to defend today. Arty Hume, who was elected in Amble ward last year with 44% of the vote against 38% for Labour and 17% for the Conservatives, died in July at the age of 71 following a career as an electrician in the mines, as a police officer and as a part-time steward for Newcastle United football club. He was described by his family following his death as "a humble, polite, respectful man that was well liked cross party in the chamber".

The same cannot be said of John Allen, who won Hirst ward for Reform UK in 2025 by the similarly narrow margin of 50-43 over Labour. Allen was thrown out of Reform UK later that year for online comments saying that "I'd shoot Starmer myself if I had the weapon and opportunity", and he then lost his seat on the council in August for failing to turn up to any meetings of the council in six months.

John Allen is seeking re-election in the Hirst ward by-election caused by his own disqualification, this time as an independent candidate. The official defending Reform UK candidate is Ashington town councillor Kerry Davison, who unsuccessfully stood for Northumberland council in Ashington Central ward last year. Another town councillor on the ballot is Labour's Melanie Fox, who is a provider of creative wellbeing workshops. Also on the Hirst ward candidate list are David Ferguson for the Conservatives, Calvin Clark for the Lib Dems and Eden Webley - who had been the Brexit Party candidate for Wansbeck in the 2019 general election - for Restore Britain.

There is a shorter ballot paper in Amble with two ladies, one man and Warren Ladyman who is the defending Reform UK candidate: he stood in the extremely far-flung Bellingham ward last year. Labour have the left half of the field all to themselves with their candidate Amy Spriggs, who will be hoping for better than her fifth-place performance in the neighbouring Amble West with Warkworth ward in 2025. The Conservatives have nominated Jenny Richards and the Restore Britain candidate is Michael Joyce.

Claremont

Blackpool council, Lancashire; caused by the death of Labour councillor Ivan Taylor.

For this week's Labour defence we're off to the Blackpool Illuminations. Claremont ward is basically Blackpool's North Shore, running from the North Pier up to the Grand Hotel (ex-Hilton), which is a strong contender for the title of ugliest building in the North of England. The seafront in between is filled with hotels, including the Metropole which is the only Blackpool hotel between the Promenade and the sea. It has recently been cleared of its asylum seekers.

The ward boundary includes the Blackpool North railway terminus which is one of the main points of entry to the town for tourists and holidaymakers. This is also now a stop for the town's trams, with a branch line opening in 2024 from the railway station to the existing seafront line at North Pier. None of this has much to do with the Claremont name: the Claremont area lies at the eastern end of this ward around Claremont Road and Claremont Park, well away from the beach.

The 2021 census return, taken when Claremont ward had slightly different boundaries to those of today, lays bare the area's social problems and its dependence on the tourist industry. Claremont ranked 5th of the wards in England and Wales for residents who were long-term sick or disabled, at 14.3% of adults; this was the highest figure for any ward in north-west England. It ranked in the top 20 wards for residents employed in the accommodation and food service sector (17.9%), in the top 70 wards for unemployment (7.5%) and in the top 80 wards for private renting (51.4% of households).

The late Labour councillor Ivan Taylor had the job of trying to address those problems for an extraordinarily long time. Taylor was one of the very few remaining councillors from before the 1974 reorganisation, having been first elected to Blackpool county borough council all the way back in 1966. He had 53 years of continuous service on Blackpool council from 1973, rising to a total of 59 years once his two non-consecutive spells on the old county borough council are added in. When he died in July at the age of 86, he was thought to be the UK's longest-serving councillor.

Ivan Taylor was leader of Blackpool council from 1991 to 2000, during which the council became a unitary authority independent of Lancashire county council, and he also served in multiple roles in the council's cabinet. He was mayor of Blackpool in 2002-03. Ivan's wife Sylvia, who died in 2021, was also his Claremont ward colleague for 20 years from 1995 to 2015, and she had her own mayoral year in 2012-13.

In all Ivan Taylor won twelve terms of office, with his final re-election for Claremont coming in 2023 alongside the present council leader Lynn Williams. On that occasion the winning Labour slate polled 52% of the vote against 22% for the Conservatives and 14% for an independent candidate.

Boundary changes for the July 2024 general election transferred Claremont ward into the Blackpool South constituency. This is represented by Labour MP Chris Webb, a Blackpool lad who had previously served for three years on Manchester city council and then for three years as deputy police and crime commissioner for Lancashire. Webb had had two previous attempts to get into Parliament for Blackpool North and Cleveleys before he won the May 2024 parliamentary by-election in Blackpool South, which did not then cover Claremont ward.

There have been no elections here since July 2024, so this is the first opportunity for the voters of Claremont ward to have their say in the political changes which have occurred during the current Labour government. Defending Ivan Taylor's seat for Labour is Katie Donnelli-Hunn, who works as a House of Commons caseworker and is standing in her first election. The Conservatives have selected John Thomson, who stood unsuccessfully in the neighbouring Brunswick ward in 2023. We have an independent candidacy from Kim Knight, who stood in the 2024 general election as the Alliance for Democracy and Freedom candidate in Blackpool South; Knight had previously stood under that banner in the 2024 Blackpool South by-election, and she has previously stood as a Reform UK candidate for Blackpool council and as the Brexit Party parliamentary candidate for Bootle in 2019. The Lib Dem candidate is Kevan Benfold; Restore Britain have nominated Paula Brooksbank; Reform UK have selected Mark Butcher, who was runner-up in Blackpool South in July 2024 and third in the by-election two months earlier when this column noted his track record of charity work in the town; and the Green Party candidate is Rob Jones.

Long Whatton and Diseworth

North West Leicestershire council; caused by the death of Conservative councillor Nick Rushton.

Our final ward today is in a location where many pass through but few stop. The villages of Long Whatton and Diseworth lie to the north-west of Loughborough and to the south-east of Derby, straddling the junction of the M1 motorway and the A42 road which is a major part of the East Midlands' infrastructure. This junction lies next to the Donington Park motorway service area and to East Midlands Airport, which this ward covers part of (although not the passenger terminal).

East Midlands, which is run by the Manchester Airport Group and thus partly owned by the ten Greater Manchester borough councils, is a reasonably busy passenger airport favoured by the low-cost airlines. Its busiest route is to Alicante, with 369,000 passengers last year. However, its main importance to the British economy is as an air cargo hub: within the UK only Heathrow handles more freight, and cargo flights regularly take off and land here from as far away as China and Alaska. As a result of this the area around East Midlands Airport is filled with business and distribution parks which have obliterated the old parish boundaries here. The boundary of this ward takes a slice through the Nestlé warehouse to the north of the runway in a rather complicated way which no longer makes any sense on the ground.

The airport has a Derby DE postcode, and there was a failed attempt a few years back to rename it after Nottingham, but this area is covered by Leicestershire county council and by the North West Leicestershire local government district. Long Whatton and Diseworth's excellent road connections to all three of the major East Midlands cities gives the area a strong commuter demographic, and for much of the 21st century this has been a heavily Conservative area. At the most recent poll for this ward in 2023, the Conservatives' Nick Rushton was re-elected with a 60-33 lead over Labour.

North West Leicestershire council is hung after the Conservatives lost overall control in 2023 after 16 years: that year's election returned 17 Labour councillors, 12 Conservatives, 5 Lib Dems and 4 independents. With 20 seats required for a majority, the Lib Dems found themselves holding the balance of power. Unusually, the North West Leicestershire branch of the Lib Dems decided to throw their lot in with the Conservatives and an anti-Labour Tory-led coalition was formed, which continues to run the council.

The district has almost the same boundaries as the North West Leicestershire parliamentary seat, a key marginal which has voted for the government at every election since its creation in 1983. Its 2010-24 Conservative MP Andrew Bridgen went somewhat mad towards the end of his term: among other controversies he got into a long legal dispute with his own family over a potato and vegetable business, and he was eventually thrown out of the Conservative Party in 2023 for antivax views. Bridgen sought re-election as an independent candidate in 2024, finished sixth out of seven candidates and lost his deposit; his vote total of 1,568 was larger than the 1,012-vote majority over the Conservatives of North West Leicestershire's new Labour MP Amanda Hack. She had been a Leicestershire county councillor, representing Braunstone, before entering Parliament.

The most recent poll here was the 2025 Leicestershire county council election, in which Long Whatton and Diseworth ward is part of the Valley division (quite which valley this is isn't obvious from the map). This was a relatively narrow Reform UK gain last year, with their candidate Michael Squires defeating Conservative county councillor Nick Rushton by 36-29; the Greens were best of the rest on 17%. Rushton was a very long-serving and high-profile figure who had been first elected to Leicestershire county council all the way back in 1989 in Ashby de la Zouch: after losing that seat in 1993 he was elected for Breedon in 2001 and continuous service since, transferring to Valley division following boundary changes in 2005. He served for nine years as deputy leader of the county council and was then leader of the county council for 12 years from 2012 until 2024, eventually standing down from the top job to undergo treatment for cancer. Rushton died in July at the age of 70.

Nick Rushton had an even longer career in local government at district council level, where he was first elected to North West Leicestershire council in the former Ivanhoe ward in 1987: he lost his seat in 1995 in Breedon ward, got it back in 1999 and had continuous service since then, transferring to Long Whatton and Diseworth following boundary changes in 2015. Rushton had served in North West Leicestershire's cabinet since 2007, and he was also active on the town council for Ashby de la Zouch which had recently voted to give him the title of honorary alderman.

Hoping to pick up some of Rushton's personal vote will be the defending Conservative candidate Craig Smith, who is the county councillor for Coalville North and was the losing Conservative parliamentary candidate here in 2024: in 2023 Smith contested Hugglescote St Mary's ward. The Labour candidate is Damilola Ojuri who has had a couple of unsuccessful elections in this district recently: in the 2025 county election she was the Labour candidate for Ashby de la Zouch, presumably following in the footsteps of Pandora Braithwaite. Also standing are Ian Bradley for the Lib Dems, Reform UK's Charles Pugsley who is the county councillor for Castle Donington and Kegworth, Drew Timms for the Greens and Simon Tunnicliffe for Restore Britain.
