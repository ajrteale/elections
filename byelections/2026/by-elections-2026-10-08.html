Five by-elections on 8th October 2026, starting with the big one:

Holborn and St Pancras

House of Commons; caused by the resignation of Labour MP Sir Keir Starmer.

Welcome to the seventh Parliamentary by-election of the 2024 Parliament, and the first in London since 2023. By coincidence, that poll also followed the resignation from Parliament of a former Prime Minister - that was Boris Johnson, whose political career ended in disgrace. Three years ago, it's Labour's turn for the Prime Minister who led them to a large majority to resign their seat well before reaching the next general election. This triggers a by-election in Holborn and St Pancras, a constituency which covers large chunks of central London and some very touristy areas. Indeed this seat is the point of entry into London for many tourists who come in by train from the North. Including me. Whenever your columnist travels to London, my exploration of the capital starts at Euston, the first and arguably the ugliest of the London Terminals. Euston station opened in 1837 as the southern terminus and headquarters of the London and Birmingham Railway, which after several mergers became the most powerful railway company in the world, the London and North Western Railway. The station name comes from the Suffolk stately home of the Duke of Grafton, who owned the land that the station was built on. The present Euston station building dates from a controversial rebuilding in the 1960s, and its 16 platforms handle intercity trains along the West Coast Main Line to the West Midlands, north-west England, north Wales (for ferry connections to Dublin), and Scotland; the Caledonian Sleeper to Scotland; and local and commuter trains to Watford, Milton Keynes, Northampton and the West Midlands. It is ranked at number 10 in the UK's list of busiest railway stations, handling over 40 million passengers in 2024-25. The western side of the station is a large building site which will hopefully become the southern terminus of High Speed 2. High Speed 1 terminates a few hundred yards to the east at St Pancras railway station, which was refurbished far more sensitively in the early 2000s. The original St Pancras was opened in 1868 as the London terminus of the Midland Railway. The station is elevated 16 feet above the level of the Euston Road; its undercroft, held up by thousands of iron pillars whose spacing was set by the length of Burton beer barrels, is now occupied by shops and by facilities for international Eurostar trains which run to and from the Continent via the Channel Tunnel. These are the only services which use the original trainshed, which is covered by a single arched roof almost 75 metres wide; when the roof was built, it was the largest structure of its kind in the world. The poet John Betjeman, whose campaigning saved the station from demolition in the 1960s, has a statue within the building by Martin Jennings, who later designed the portrait of Charles III used on the UK's coins. St Pancras is effectively four stations in one, with dedicated sets of platforms for international trains, intercity trains to the East Midlands and Yorkshire, domestic trains to Kent via High Speed 1, and the busy platforms for the Thameslink route at subsurface level. The whole complex is the UK's 11th-busiest station, with 38.8 million pasengers in 2024-25. The building of St Pancras station effectively wiped out a slum neighbourhood called Agar Town, while the buliding of its approach lines disturbed the graveyard of St Pancras Old Church. The Midland Railway paid for the relocation of the dead in the churchyard - the writer Thomas Hardy was put in charge of the exhumations - but they did not pay for the relocation of the living in the slum housing. St Pancras Old Church is certainly of great antiquity, although claims that it was a Roman centre of worship in the 4th century lack strong evidence to support them. The present building was largely rebuilt in the 19th century. If the church does go back to AD 314 as claimed, then it would have been established not long afer the death of the original St Pancras, a Christian martyr who was born in what is now Turkey and was beheaded in Rome during the Diocletion persecution in 303 or 304, aged 14. His name comes from the Greek Pankratios, meaning "all-powerful". The old St Pancras Church was little used by the start of the 19th century, with most divine services in its ancient parish taking place in Kentish Town where most of the local population lived at the time: the area around the old church was subject to flooding from the lost River Fleet. Subsequent population growth around the Euston Road led to the building of St Pancras New Church, which opened in 1822 and is a massive Greek Revival pile. It's so clearly modelled on the Acropolis in Athens that it has caryatids. Further along the south side of the Euston Road, and lying directly opposite the Gothic St Pancras station hotel, is the former St Pancras Town Hall which dates from 1937 and is built in the distinctive 1930s Municipal style. It is still in municipal use today by the successor local authority. The Euston Road itself was laid out in 1756 as part of the New Road, a turnpike route along which livestock could be driven from Paddington to Smithfield Market: the idea was to avoid the existing route via Oxford Street and High Holborn, which was extremely congested even then.

The name of Holborn and St Pancras recalls local government units which ceased to exist six decades ago, when the Metropolitan Boroughs of Hampstead, Holborn and St Pancras merged into a single local government unit as part of the creation of Greater London. The new London Borough did not take any of these names: instead it became known by a name which has become associated with this area by a rather curious route.

The story starts back in 1575, when a Londoner called William Camden with a passion for antiquarianism got a job as the Usher of Westminster School. This was a post which gave him a lot of free time during the school holidays, which Camden used to travel extensively around the British Isles. The result of this travel was Britannia, which is described on its front page as a chorographica descriptio. "Chorography" comes from the Greek words for writing about places: the ancient Greek geographer Ptolemy used the word to refer to the study of smaller parts of the word, as opposed to geography as the study of the world as a whole. Unfortunately, since Camden's day the word "chorography" has fallen out of the English language in favour of "topography" or "cartography", neither of which are really a good description of what William Camden was trying to do. His aim in writing Britannia was to describe the Britain of his day, county by county, by linking together its landscape, geography, ancient history and modern history. Andrew's Previews is proud to follow in that tradition.

First published in 1586, Britannia was a huge success and continued to be expanded until its sixth edition in 1607; it was originally written in Latin, and its first English translation in 1610 also contained new material from Camden. In 1593 Camden moved on from Westminster School, where by this time he was headmaster, to become Clarenceux King of Arms - a post which was intended to give him more free time for research, as at this time the College of Arms was noted for being a centre of antiquarian study. It was also noted for its unusually vicious internal politics, which Camden navigated successfully. Camden's Annales, a history of Elizabeth I's reign, dates from this period; his post as Clarenceux King of Arms meant that he attended the queen's funeral in 1603. William Camden made enough money out of his works to move to a country house in Chislehurst, Kent, in 1609: that was the year he sat for the portrait above by Marcus Gheraerts the Younger. In 1622, the year before he died, he established a chair at Oxford which is now the Camden Professorship of Ancient History at Brasenose College.

Our story now turns to the eighteenth-century figure of Charles Pratt, who was a confidant of William Pitt the Elder (they had been at Eton together) and had a noted legal and political career: he served in Cabinet for a total of fifteen years under five different Prime Ministers. Pratt only served as an MP for four years, representing the tiny borough of Downton in Wiltshire from 1757 to 1761. During that time his legal career blossomed and that gave him the financial security to buy Camden Place - William Camden's old country house in Chislehurst.

In 1761 Pratt swapped the Commons benches for the judicial benches and became Chief Justice of the Common Pleas, receiving first the customary knighthood and then being elevated to the Lords in 1765. He took the title Lord Camden, after his country seat. He quickly became what we would now call an activist judge, freeing the radical MP John Wilkes from arrest on the grounds of parliamentary privilege and giving the leading judgment in the 1765 case of Entick v Carrington, which established that the State can only lawfully do what the common law or the statutes allow it to do.

Some time later Entick v Carrington became one of the main inspirations for the Fourth Amendment to the US Constitution, part of the Bill of Rights - which is ironic, given that Lord Camden ended up on the losing side of the American War of Independence. In 1766 Pitt the Elder appointed him to the Cabinet as Lord Chancellor, in which post Camden served for four years before being forced out in 1770 over his opposition to government policies on America and John Wilkes. He returned to Cabinet as Lord President of the Council for a short period in 1782-83 and then again in 1784 after Pitt the Younger came to power. Now promoted to Earl Camden, he remained as Lord President until his death in 1794.

Through his wife, Earl Camden owned some land not far to the north of London. In 1791 he granted leases for the development of 1,400 homes on this land; thus was born the settlement of Camden Town, which many years later gave its name to the London Borough of Camden. In the 1820s Camden Road was laid out, running in a straight line north-east from Camden Town to Holloway Road. Parallel to and to the south of Camden Road can be found a leafy development of high-status detached houses, around the green space of Camden Square.

Camden Square has a number of associations with politics, particularly towards the radical and pro-independence end of the political spectrum. In 1914 the suffragette Emmeline Pankhurst, then a fugitive under the Cat and Mouse Act, addressed her supporters in the square from a balcony. Number 52 was the home of another suffragist, Annie Swan, who went on to become one of the Liberal Party's first female parliamentary candidates when she contested Glasgow Maryhill in the 1922 general election; Swan subsequently became a founding member of the Scottish National Party. 52 Camden Square is now part of the London Irish Centre and has played host to all sorts of well-known Irish figures from Gerry Adams to Shane MacGowan.

At 57 Camden Square we find a plaque in honour of V K Krishna Menon, the founder of the Penguin imprint Pelican Books, campaigner for Indian independence and, subsequently, the first Indian High Commissioner to the UK; Menon later served in Nehru's cabinet and in both houses of the Indian parliament, but his career in elected office started right here on St Pancras council on which he sat as a Labour councillor from 1934 to 1947. Finally, at the north end of the square we find 1 South Villas which was home for many years to the West African Students' Union, where figures such as Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta were regular visitors before leading their countries to independence.

Still waiting to become eligible for its blue plaque is 30 Camden Square, the former home of the musician Amy Winehouse, who entered the notorious "27 Club" when she died there in 2011 from an alcohol overdose. In 2020 Camden council had to put out a notice asking Winehouse fans not to steal the Camden Square roadsigns as memorabilia; at least 14 replacement signs had to be put up between 2011 and 2020, costing council taxpayers £290 each to replace.

Camden Square was disturbed in the 1860s by the development of the Midland railway line into St Pancras station, which opened in 1868; the railway runs underneath the Square in a tunnel. This line's first railway station north of St Pancras is at Kentish Town, whose name has nothing to do with the county of Kent: Kentish Town lies on the lost River Fleet, and the second part of the name is thought to be related to the modern word "ditch" and to refer to that river. (Those from the Garden of England are traditionally divided by the River Medway into either Kentish Men or Men of Kent; a foolproof way to remember which way round this goes is to note that Kentish Town is on the correct side of the Medway.) Kentish Town is normally a busy interchange between Thameslink trains and the Northern line of the London Underground: but the Tube station has been closed since 2023 for replacement of its escalators and refurbishment work, which is overrunning its original completion date of the summer just gone.

The nearby South Kentish Town underground station closed a century ago due to low usage, becoming one of London Underground's "ghost" stations; it is still maintained as an emergency exit from the system. John Betjeman wrote a short story in the 1950s about a train stopping there after the station's closure and opening its doors in error; a passenger then alighted absent-mindedly, and couldn't find his way out. If you want to experience that feeling for yourself, you can: the old South Kentish Town station building is now in use as an escape room.

London - then defined as the area of the Metropolitan Board of Works - was the biggest winner in seat terms from the 1885 redistribution, which made single-member constituencies the norm for the first time. Middlesex alone went up from 18 MPs to 47, and altogether the MBW area was divided into 59 constituencies. This figure goes to show just how densely populated inner London was then. In 1885 Holborn was a constituency of its own (one of three seats covering the parliamentary borough of Finsbury), and St Pancras was divided into four constituencies called St Pancras East, North, South and West. Those names are positively imaginative compared with the wards drawn up for St Pancras borough council on its creation in 1900: those wards didn't have names at all, instead being numbered 1 to 8 from north to south. All four St Pancras constituencies voted Liberal at their first election in 1885, before swinging more or less in tune with the national tide. In its first incarnation St Pancras North covered western Kentish Town and Tufnell Park, together with Gospel Oak and Highgate which are no longer in this constituency. Its first MP was Thomas Bolton, a Grays Inn solicitor, who defeated the Conservatives' Charles Cochrane-Baillie by 55-45. Cochrane-Baillie then won the rematch in 1886. In 1890 Charles Cochrane-Baillie succeeded to his father's titles and entered the House of Lords as the second Lord Lamington, also inheriting a large estate in Lanarkshire. Thomas Bolton then narrowly got his seat back at the resulting first St Pancras North by-election of 4th March 1890, defeating the new Conservative candidate Henry Graham by 108 votes - a majority which reduced further to 60 votes in 1892. Lord Lamington then spent significant amounts of time out of the UK as Governor of Queensland in 1896-1901 and then Governor of Bombay in 1903-07, and his first governorship left a culinary delicacy: the lamington cake, a popular Australian dessert consisting of sponge cake coated with chocolate sauce and coconut, is reputed to be named after him. After defecting to the Liberal Unionists in or around 1893, Thomas Bolton retired at the 1895 general election at which St Pancras North was gained by the Conservatives' Edward Moon. It took until the 1906 landslide for the Liberals to get the seat back with their candidate Willoughby Dickinson, a Parliamentary draftsman who had been chair of the London County Council in 1900-01. In his first term of office Dickinson was a strong supporter of women's suffrage and he introduced two bills on the subject. Following his re-elections by easy margins in both 1910 elections (his Conservative opponent in January was the wonderfully-named Hercules Pakenham, who would later serve in the Northern Ireland Senate) Dickinson sat on the Speaker's Conference on electoral reform, which recommended giving the vote to women, and he was sworn of the Privy Council in 1914. The St Pancras East constituency of 1885-1918 ran along the Midland railway line from Kentish Town to Somers Town, with its boundaries being defined by the Euston Road, Eversholt Street, Camden High Street, Kentish Town Road, Leighton Road and York Way. Large amounts of its acreage which taken up by goods depots for the Great Northern Railway and the Midland Railway, whose Kings Cross and St Pancras termini were part of the seat. East was narrowly won at its first election in 1885 by the Liberals' Thomas Gibb, the vestry clerk of St Pancras. Gibb was rather soundly defeated in 1886 by the Conservatives' Robert Webster, a barrister who wrote a number of books on subjects including world trade and the law relating to canals - which must have been helpful for his time in the Commons, given the presence of the Regent's Canal in his constituency. After leaving Parliament Webster wrote a 1907 book with the title of Elections, Electors and Elected: Stories of Elections Past and Present, which I must try and get hold of a copy of. Robert Webster resigned from the Commons in 1899 to go back to his legal career. The resulting St Pancras East by-election was narrowly held for the Conservatives by Thomas Wrightson, who returned to the Commons after losing his seat in Stockton in 1895: he held off the Liberal candidate Benjamin Costelloe, a London county councillor for Bethnal Green South East, by 187 votes. Wrightson was re-elected more comfortably in 1900, and he was also made a baronet in that year. In 1906 Sir Thomas Wrightson was defeated in the Liberal landslide by Hugh Lea, a newspaper proprietor who sttod down after one term. Lea stood down after one term and passed St Pancras East on to Joseph Martin, who had previously had a long political career in his native Canada. Martin was briefly prime minister of British Columbia in 1900, represented Winnipeg in the Canadian House of Commons from 1893 to 1896 and also sat for nine years in the Legislative Assembly of Manitoba. He was also a member of the Canadian Bar, and he had come to the UK to practise before the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council which was then the final court appeal for Canada. He won St Pancras East easily enough in both 1910 elections, but then fell out with his constituency party and fought off a deslection attempt in 1914 by threatening to resign his seat and stgand in the resulting by-election as an independent Lib-Lab candidate. Martin ended up defecting to Labour, and he stood as a Labour candidate in Islington in 1918 after St Pancras East disappeared in boundary changes. Meanwhile the St Pancras West constituency was dominated by the London and North Western Railway, taking in the passenger terminus at Euston and the large goods depot at Primrose Hill. Camden Town, which was much more industrial at this time, was also part of the seat together with the eastern fringe of Regent's Park. In 1885 St Pancras West became the first of a large number of seats represented by the Liberals' Harry Levy-Lawson, who was just 23 at the time. He was from the family which owned and ran the Daily Telegraph, which was rather less right-wing in those days. Levy-Lawson narrowly held on to his seat in 1886 by 64 votes, before going down to defeat in 1892 by 42 votes. His next electoral contest, an October 1892 by-election in Cirencester, was even closer: Levy-Lawson lost by 3 votes to the Conservatives' Thomas Chester-Master and took the result to the Election Court, which after a recount and scrutiny ruled that the 1892 Cirencester by-election had ended with a tied result and ordered a fresh by-election. All this was facilitated by St Pancras West voting in 1892 for the Conservatives' Harry Graham, who would go on to serve for 14 years before being swept away in the Liberal landslide. This brought to the Commons Sir William Collins, who had a prominent medical career as a surgeon despite holding antivax views which were unusual even then. Collins had been chairman of the London County Council in 1897-98 and he was the first chair of the LCC's education commitee. He had been knighted in Edward VII's Coronation honours in 1902. During his first Commons term he became vice-chancellor of the University of London. The two 1910 elections in St Pancras West both finished on a knife-edge. Collins held his seat in January just 10 votes ahead of the Conservatives, before losing his seat in December by the even narrower margin of 9 votes. This brought into the Commons the Conservative MP Felix Cassel, a barrister who was the nephew of the banker and philanthropist Sir Ernest Cassel. Despite his German background - he had been born into a Jewish family in Cologne - Felix Cassel fought in the First World War on the British side before being appointed in 1916 as Judge Advocate-General, responsible for courts-martial in the Army. Cassel then left the Commons to take up his new role, and the resulting St Pancras West by-election of 16th October 1916 was won unopposed, thanks to the wartime political truce, by the Conservatives' Richard Barnett. A barrister by trade, Barnett was an Irishman from County Down who had competed in the shooting events at the 1908 London Olympics, finishing fourth in the 1,000 yard free rifle competition; he was also a strong chess player who had won the Irish championship in 1886. He was serving on the Western Front at the time of his election, and was mentioned in despatches in 1917; Barnett finished the war with the rank of captain. The tiny but densely populated St Pancras South constituency of 1885-1918 covered the part of St Pancras borough south of the Euston Road, running from Fitzrovia in the west to the King's Cross Road to the east. Then, as now, the seat was dominated by University College London and its first MP was strongly connected with that university. In 1885 this was the last of several constituencies represented by Sir Julian Goldsmid, 3rd Baronet, who had previously represented Honiton in 1866-68 and then Rochester from 1870 to 1880. He was originally elected in St Pancras South for the Liberals, but in 1886 he defected to the breakaway Liberal Unionists over the issue of Irish home rule. Under his new political colours Sir Julian held St Pancras South until his death in 1896, by which point he was UCL's vice-chancellor. Following his 1880 defeat in Rochester, Goldsmid then tried to get straight back into Parliament by contesting a May 1880 by-election in Sandwich which was corrupt from top to bottom. The Election Court voided the result on Goldsmid's petition, and a subsequent Royal Commission found that over 900 of the 2,115 electors had taken bribes and over 100 people had given bribes. Parliament refused to issue a writ for a second Sandwich by-election, and the town was disenfranchised in 1885 as a punishment for its corruption. The St Pancras South by-election of 28th January 1896, following Sir Julian Goldsmid's death, was won by the new Liberal Unionist candidate Herbert Jessel who was Goldsmid's son-in-law. His father Sir George Jessel had risen to become Solicitor-General and Master of the Rolls, but Herbert had made his career as an Army officer before entering politics: before too long he was back in uniform serving in South Africa during the Boer War. Jessel was also a Westminster city councillor and he was mayor of Westminster in 1902-03. It took the 1906 Liberal landlside to lever Herbert Jessel out of office, and even then he only lost by 61 votes to Philip Wilson, a Cumbrian-born journalist who had been one of the first editors of Granta. Wilson only served on term, as Jessel comfortably regained St Pancras South for the Liberal Unionists in January 1910. The final piece of the puzzle in the 1885-1918 period was the even smaller Holborn constituency, which covered an area of just over 400 acres - even smaller than the City of London. The constituency was created in 1885 as one of three divisions covering the parliamentary borough of Finsbury, but in 1900 it became an independent local government unit as the smallest of the County of London's 28 metropolitan boroughs. Its population fell consistently during this period, from over 70,000 in 1885 to just under 25,000 in 1951. The Holborn constituency covered a diverse area between Westminster and the City, including two of the four Inns of Court (Lincoln's Inn and Gray's Inn), the jewellery quarter around Hatton Garden, the fashionable Georgian housing of Bloomsbury and the rather less fashionable slum housing of St Giles. It's fair to say that St Giles was consistently outvoted, because Holborn was a safe Conservative seat throughout its existence. Its first election set the tone: the Conservaives' Francis Duncan, a Royal Artillery officer, won with 62% of the vote and a majority of 1,574 votes over the Liberal candidate. Duncan increased his majority in 1886 when the Liberal candidate was Dadabhai Naoroji, who had just become one of the founding members of the Indian National Congress; Daoroji subsequently served as MP for Finsbury Central in 1892-95. Francis Duncan died in office in 1888. The resulting first Holborn by-election of 29th November 1888 was won by the Conservative candidate Gainsford Bruce, who finally made it into Parliament at his fifth attempt. He was a QC specialising in shipping law. Gainford Bruce left the Commons just after being re-elected in 1892, to become a High Court judge. The resulting second Holborn by-election of 12th August 1892, the first by-election of the 1892-95 Parliament, was won without a contest by Sir Charles Hall, a lawyer specialising in Admiralty matters who was naking a quick return to Parliament after being defeated in Chesterton at the general election. Hall also became Recorder of London in that year, an appointment which required him to resign as attorney-general to the future Edward VII. Nobody opposed his re-election to the Commons in 1895. Sir Charles Hall died in 1900 at the age of 56. The resulting third Holborn by-election of 23rd March 1900 returned James Remnant, yet another barrister who was a London county councillor for Holborn and chaired the LCC's theatre committee. He was unopposed at both the by-election and the 1900 general election shortly afterwards, setting up a 28-year career on the Conservative backbenches. Renmant was made a baronet in 1917, and eventually swapped the green benches for the red benches in 1928 when he was created as the first Lord Remnant. The resulting fourth Holborn by-election of 28th June 1928 exposed a split in the local Conservatives, with a large faction of the local party opposing the selection of Stuart Bevan as the new Conservative candidate and threatening to stand an independent candidate against him; however, no independent candidacy was forthcoming and Bevan won the by-election very comfortably. He was a bencher of the Middle Temple, and he became Recorder of Bristol in 1932. Stuart Bevan died in October 1935 at the age of 63, on the day that Parliament was dissolved for that year's general election. His seat was duly filled at the general election by Sir Robert Tasker, who - in a break from the pattern of Holborn's MPs being barristers - was an architect. Tasker was a long-serving London county councillor who was chairman of the LCC in 1930-31; he had previously sat in the 1924-29 Parliament as the Conservative MP for Islington East. Smewhat to his own surprise, Sir Robert Tasker was deselected for the 1945 general election in favour of Max Aitken, who was the son and heir of Lord Beaverbrook and his Daily Express newspaper empire. Aitken had spent the war as an RAF officer and ace fighter pilot, credited with 16½ kills and winning the DSO and DFC. He won Holborn by the unusually narrow margin of 925 votes over the Labour candidate Irene Marcousé, who shortly afterwards became the first Labour leader of Holborn council. The Holborn constituency had survived the 1918 redistribution more or less intact, but that year's boundary changes had reduced St Pancras borough from four MPs to three. The seat which disappeared was St Pancras South, which got divided between the East and West constituencies to create new St Pancras South East and South West seats. In the case of St Pancras South West, the Fitzrovia area - then covered by St Pancras ward number 7 - was a straightforward addition to the old St Pancras West. The Conservatives' Richard Barnett, who had taken over St Pancras West in a wartime 1916 by-election, easily achieved re-election in South West in 1918 with the government coupon and went on to win three further terms of office in the 1922-24 general elections; he was helped in 1923 by the left-wing vote being evenly split between the Liberals and Labour. Barnett was knighted in 1925. Sir Richard Barnett retired at the 1929 election, in which St Pancras South West voted Labour for the first time: the Conservative candidate Patrick Spens, who would later serve as the last chief justice of British India, lost by 1,779 votes to Labour's William Carter who was a railway union official - a helpful connection in a seat which included Euston. Carter was also a St Pancras councillor and he had served as mayor of the borough in 1919-20. William Carter was swept away in 1931 on a huge swing to the Conservatives of over 21%, which delivered a crushing win for the Conservative candidate George Mitcheson. Mitcheson was re-elected rather more narrowly in 1935, and was knighted shortly afterwards in the 1936 New Year honours for political and public service. He retired from the Commons in 1945. St Pancras South East had a more complicated history. Compared to the old St Pancras East, it gained the King's Cross area (ward 8) but lost Kentish Town (the part of ward 3 north of the Camden Road), which was transferred to St Pancras North. Its first contest in 1918 was equally complicated, with all four candidates achieving the 12½% required to save their deposits amid some confusion as to who, if anyone, had the government coupon. In the end the winner was the Conservatives' John Hopkins, a civil engineer, with second place going to the Liberal candidate Richard Reiss, third place to independent candidate Percy Adams who had secured the Liberal nomination for the old St Pancras East in 1917 but was then displaced by Reiss on his return from the war, and fourth place went to Labour candidate Herbert Romeril. Romeril moved into second place in St Pancras South East in 1922, before defeating John Hopkins at the 1923 general election by 692 votes. He worked at the Railway Clearing House, located just outside the constituency boundary on Eversholt Street near Euston station, where he was a union official. In the 1924 election Romeril lost his seat back to the Conservatives' John Hopkins, but he returned to the Commons in 1929. The 1931 Conservative landslide saw St Pancras South East gained by Sir Alfred Beit, 2nd Baronet, who came from a South African mining family: on the death of his father the previous year Sir Alfred had inherited not only a baronetcy but also a large fortune and art collection. Beit was narrowly re-elected in 1935 ahead of Labour candidate Santo Jeger, and he was still young enough to join the Royal Air Force when the Second World War broke out and serve in Bomber Command. The expanded St Pancras North swung in the same way as St Pancras South East, and the Conservative win here in 1918 was facilitated by the Tory candidate John Lorden getting the coupon ahead of the outgoing Liberal MP Sir Willoughby Dickinson, who was defeated. Lorden was a Wandsworth councillor who had twice served as mayor of that borough, in 1903-04 and 1907-08, and during the First Wold War he had chaired a section of a local military tribunal. He was re-elected in 1922 at the top of a three-way marginal result. Lorden was defeated at the 1923 general election by Labour's James Marley, a Scotsman who had moved to London to take up a teaching position in Hampstead. Marley served only one term before losing his seat in 1924 to the Conservatives' Ian Fraser. Fraser lost his seat back to Marley in 1929, but got it back in the 1931 Tory landslide and was re-elected for a third term in 1935. Ian Fraser was a businessman who had lost his sight to a German bullet on the Somme, and after the war he had become chairman of St Dunstan's, the charity for blind ex-servicemen: he would go on to hold that position for over half a century. Fraser was knighted in 1934 for his work at St Dunstan's. He combined his business career with a long political career - many years later, Fraser was the first person to receive a life peerage under the Life Peerages Act 1958 - but this was temporarily interrupted in 1936 when Fraser was appointed to the BBC board of governors. This required him to leave the Commons. The resulting second St Pancras North by-election of 4th February 1937 was narrowly won by the defending Conservative candidate Robert Grant Ferris, who was an officer in the Royal Auxiliary Air Force. He served in the senior RAF during the Second World War, and he was one of the first airmen to receive the Air Efficiency Award on its creation in 1942. In the same year Ferris added a hyphen to his name by deed poll, becoming Robert Grant-Ferris. All three St Pancras constituencies were gained by Labour in the 1945 Attlee landslide, with Robert Grant-Ferris losing his seat to George House. House was a former secretary of the Constructional Engineering Union, and he also represented St Pancras North on the London County Council. George House died in 1949 at the age of 56. The resulting third St Pancras North by-election of 10th March 1949 was won comfortably enough by Labour candidate Kenneth Robinson, an insurance broker and St Pancras councillor who had served in the Royal Navy during the Second World War, rising in rank from ordinary seaman to lieutenant-commander in five years. The 1945 election in St Pancras South East was a rematch of the 1935 contest between the Conservative MP Sir Alfred Beit and Labour candidate Santo Jeger, a doctor who had spent many years in local government before entering the Commons. Jeger had been first elected to Shoreditch council in 1925, serving as mayor of Shoreditch in 1929-30, and he had sat on the London County Council since 1931. He had provided medical aid to the republican side in the Spanish civil war. St Pancras South West was gained in the 1945 election by Labour's Haydn Davies, a Welshman who had come to London to work as a teacher. Many years previously, in 1929, Davies had contested St Pancras South West as the Liberal candidate. He served only one term in the Commons before being displaced by boundary changes, and he ended up seeking re-election in 1950 in York without success. The population of Central London in general fell significantly in the first half of the 20th century, as improved transport links made commuting into central London from the outer suburbs easier while slum clearance - whether municipally led, or of the more radical type effected in the 1940s by the Luftwaffe Town Planning Department - reduced the density of housing in the centre. By the time of the 1950 redistribution - the first redrawing of the parliamentary boundaries since 1918 - London had become severely over-represented due to a combination of all these factors. The opportunity was taken to abolish or merge a large number of inner-city seats, and Holborn and St Pancras did not escape this process. The combined area of the two boroughs went down from four MPs to two, with the abolition of St Pancras South East and St Pancras South West. The southern parts of those seats (covering Regsnt's Park, Euston, Somers Town and the railway termini) were added to the Holborn constituency to create a new seat called Holborn and St Pancras South, while St Pancras North expanded to take in the Camden Town and Camden Square areas. The 1950 boundary changes turned St Pancras North into a safe Labour seat and set the 1949 by-election winner Kenneth Robinson up for a long career in Parliament. When Labour came to power in 1964 Robinson joined the Wilson Cabinet as minister of health, serving in that position for four years; in 1968 he was reshuffled to become minister for planning and land before leaving the frontbench in 1969. Robinson had long campaigned on health and mental health issues, and his four years as minister of health saw the negotiation of a GPs' charter and the passage of the Abortion Act 1967, which legalised abortion in Great Britain by registered practioners on certain grounds and made abortion available on the NHS. He had also campaigned for suicide to be decriminalised, which eventually happened in 1961, and he was a supporter of the Wolfenden report which had recommended the decriminalisation of gay sex between consenting adults. Robinson had also served as chairman of the National Association for Mental Health, the charity now known as Mind, and this led to a conflict with Scientologists after he denounced them in Parliament as a cult: the Church of Scientology responded by publishing defamatory articles about Robinson, for which they ended up having to pay him substantial libel damages. Kenneth Robinson retired from the Commons in 1970 and passed the St Pancras North seat on to the new Labour MP Albert "Jock" Stallard, a Camden councillor who had also served as an alderman and councillor on the previous St Pancras council. Stallard was a government whip for most of the Callaghan government, when Labour had little or no majority in the Commons. In both 1974 elections his Conservative opponent was a thirtysomething banker from Lambeth called John Major, whose February 1974 campaign was his début as a Parliamentary candidate. The Holborn and St Pancras South seat was more closely contested from its creation in 1950, but Labour always had the edge here and the Conservatives only won the seat once, in the Macmillan landslide of 1959. Its first MP was Labour's Santo Jeger, who took the expanded seat over after previously representing St Pancras South East from 1945; of the other predecessor seats, Haydn Davies of St Pancras South West uncessfully contested York as already stated, while Holborn's Conservative MP Max Aitken retired; Jeger ended up with a majority of 1,230 votes over the Conservatives in 1950. In 1951 the Conservatives put up the former Nottingham MP Louis Gluckstein, who was a political giant in the sense that he was at the time the tallest person ever elected to the Commons at 6 feet and 8 inches; Santo Jeger increased the Labour majority, against the national trend. Santo Jeger died in 1953 at the age of 55. The resulting Holborn and St Pancras South by-election of 19th November 1953 saw a further swing to Labour, who selected his widow Lena Jeger: she was a London county councillor and St Pancras councillor, who worked as a journalist for what was then still the Manchester Guardian. Lena increased the Labour majority to 1,976 votes, and she was more narrowly re-elected for a full term in 1955 as one of the very few women MPs in the Commons at the time: of the 630 MPs elected in 1955, only 24 were women. For the 1959 election in Holborn and St Pancras South the Conservatives selected Geoffrey Johnson-Smith, a former London county councillor who had a high public profile at the time as a TV presenter. Shortly before the election Johnson-Smith had been promoted to temporary co-host of the BBC's Tonight programme, deputising for the show's regular presenter Cliff Michelmore who was recovering from a hernia operation. On election night his BBC colleagues got to report the election of one of their own: in a straight fight Johnson-Smith defeated Lena Jeger by 17,065 votes to 16,409, a Conservative majority of 656. In his first term in Parliament Geoffrey Johnson-Smith successfully piloted a private member's bill, the National Assistance (Amendment) Act 1962, which enabled local councils to provide meals-on-wheels services. But his majority was too small to expect a long parliamentary career in Holborn and St Pancras, and Lena Jeger got her seat back in 1964 with a majority of 2,576. Johnson-Smith got back into a Parliament the following year by winning a by-election in East Grinstead, and that seat did set him up for a long parliamentary career: he eventually retired in 2001. Lena Jeger went on to be re-elected four more times in Holborn and St Pancras, combining her MP role from 1968 with membership of the Labour Party NEC. She eventually retired in 1979 and passed what become a safe Labour seat on to Frank Dobson. Dobson had previously served from 1973 to 1975 as leader of Camden council, which had replaced Holborn, St Pancras and Hampstead councils in 1965, before standing down from the council to take up a politically restricted job with the Local Government Ombudsman. Following further boundary changes in 1983 Frank Dobson became the first MP for the present Holborn and St Pancras constituency, which was created to reduce the number of MPs for Camden from three to two. This resulted in the dissolution of the St Pancras North constituency, with Highgate being added to the Hampstead constituency (which was renamed Hampstead and Highgate) while the rest of the St Pancras North seat was transferred into Holborn and St Pancras South to create a seat with similar boundaries to those of today, the main difference being that the 1983 seat included Gospel Oak. Highgate was transferred into the Holborn and St Pancras seat in 2010, but this change was reversed in 2024. To date Holborn and St Pancras has been a safe Labour seat which has only had two MPs in the last 43 years. Frank Dobson took the expanded seat over in 1983 and used it as a springboard for his long frontbench career: he was a fixture of the Labour Shadow Cabinet from 1987 onwards, and took over the high-profile position of health secretary in Tony Blair's first cabinet in 1997, serving in Cabinet for two years. In 2000 Dobson was the Labour candidate in the first election for mayor of London, finishing in third place between the Conservatives' Steve Norris and the winning independent candidate Ken Livingstone, whom Dobson had controversially defeated for the Labour nomination. Frank Dobson eventually retired from the Commons in 2015, having served for 36 years, and the Labour nomination to succeed him went to Sir Keir Starmer who was coming rather late to elected politics - he was aged 52 at the time. Appropriately for a seat containing two of the Inns of Court, Starmer had made his career in the law: he was a barrister specialising in human rights cases, and he then served from 2008 to 2013 as Director of Public Prosecutions, heading the Crown Prosecution Service. This was effectively a senior civil service role, and following his retirement Starmer became a knight of the Order of the Bath - an honour which is traditionally reserved for senior civil servants and high-ranking military officers - in the 2014 New Year Honours list. In May 2015 he was elected as Labour MP for Holborn and St Pancras with 53% of the vote, against 22% for the Conservatives and 13% for the then leader of the Green Party, Natalie Bennett. With such a prominent previous CV, it was only natural that Sir Keir Starmer would quickly rise to the Labour frontbench. In September 2015 he was appointed by Jeremy Corbyn as shadow immigration minister, and he then served in the Shadow Cabinet from October 2016 onwards as shadow Brexit secretary - staying in that role throughout the turbulent years of the 2017-19 Parliament which were dominated by that single issue. Following the Labour defeat at the 2019 general election and the resignation of Jeremy Corbyn as party leader, Sir Keir Starmer won the 2020 Labour leadership election with 56% of the vote against Rebecca Long-Bailey and Lisa Nandy, and he served as leader of the opposition through the rest of the COVID-hit 2019-24 Parliament. Once the Conservatives' polling position had disintegrated through a combination of controversy and chronic incompetence, it was only a matter of time before Sir Keir became Prime Minister - which he did following the massive Labour win in the 2024 general election. The Starmer government proved to be a disappointment for many of its supporters, and Sir Keir eventually resigned from the top job two years in rather than face a leadership challenge from Andy Burnham following the latter's win in June's Makerfield by-election. Once Parliament reconvened following the summer recess, Sir Keir Starmer tendered his resignation from the Commons - giving the scoop to his local newspaper, the Camden New Journal. Rumour has it that he is interested in a new job on the world stage, with the Secretary-Generalship of NATO having been mentioned. We can see a hint of what was to come for the Starmer administration in Sir Keir's personal result in Holborn and St Pancras in 2024. He won very comfortably with 49% of the vote in a field of 12 candidates, and a numerical majority of 11,572 votes; but party leaders usually get a boost in their own constituency at general election time, and that didn't happen here. After taking account of boundary changes, Starmer's percentage score fell by around 17 percentage points from the 2019 election. Most of this fall was to the benefit of candidates to the left of the Labour Party. Second place in Holborn and St Pancras in 2024 went to Andrew Feinstein, who polled 19% of the vote standing as a left-wing and pro-Gaza independent candidate; third place went to the Green Party on 10%. By the time of the May 2026 Camden council elections Labour's national polling situation had significantly deterioriated to the benefit of the Green Party, who won control of several inner London councils and fought Labour all the way in Camden. If we add up the votes cast across the ten Camden wards wholly in this seat then we get 39% for Labour, 29% for the Greens and 9% for Reform UK who were best of the rest, but this doesn't tell the whole story. Second and more importantly, the Greens stood down in King's Cross ward and in St Pancras and Somers Town ward in favour of the Camden People's Alliance, a new left-wing group associated with the 2024 Feinstein campaign: they ended up with 6.3% of the total votes cast across the ten wards. With the exception of King's Cross where Labour held off the Camden People's Alliance fairly comfortably, all the wards in the seat were closely contested in a way that wasn't reflected by England's first-past-the-post system. Across the Holborn and St Pancrase constituency Labour won 19 council seats plus whatever contribution you credit from Primrose Hill, against seven for the Greens and one (in St Pancras and Somers Town) for the Camden People's Alliance. One of the seven Green councillors turned out to be ineligible to stand because he worked for the council as a secondary school teacher: this triggered an immediate by-election in Regent's Park ward in July, which the new Green candidate lost to Labour. The Green ex-councillor in question was not at all happy with his party for putting him in that position, and he ended up flouncing out of the Greens in a huff amid a torrent of letters to the local newspaper, the Camden New Journal. Myatt's Fields

Lambeth council, London; caused by the death of Labour councillor Annie Gallop. We'll stay in London for the first of the polls on today's undercard, which is in a borough that is already on its third by-election of the 2026-30 term. John Betjeman once described Myatt's Fields as a "strangely beautiful place": this is a 14-acre Victorian park within the urban sprawl of south London, postally within Camberwell but administered as part of the borough of Lambeth. The park was opened in 1889 by the London County Council following a donation of land from William Minet, who also paid for a nearby library that still bears his name. Myatt's Fields Park is in the eastern part of Myatt's Fields ward, which extends north-west from here into Kennington: the ward's western and northern boundaries are the Brixton Road and the Camberwell New Road. Oval underground station lies just off the north-west corner. Myatt's Fields ward was formed in 2022 as a cut-down version of the former Vassall ward, which was named after the early 19th-century Whig politician Henry Vassall-Fox, the 3rd Lord Holland. He served in the Cabinet for three periods, as Lord Privy Seal during the Ministry of All the Talents in 1806-07, and much later as Melbourne's Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster from 1830 until his death in 1840 (with a short break from 1834-35 after William IV had dismissed Melbourne in favour of the short-lived first Peel administration). Lord Holland combined this with owning and developing significant amounts of London property: the area of Holland Park in Kensington took its name from his family, and in the 1820s he had made the first significant housing developments in the Myatt's Fields area. There is still a Vassall Road in the ward. Henry Vassall-Fox was also a slave owner, and in 1836 the UK government paid him £2,211 13s 9d to settle three compensation claims for the freedom of his slaves in Jamaica. In light of this, one of Lambeth council's objectives in the last boundary review was to erase the name of Vassall from its ward map. Having a ward named after a slaveowner was particularly inappropriate here, because of the area's ethnic makeup. In the 2021 census Myatt's Fields ward was in the top 20 wards in England and Wales for black population (36.3%) and the top 40 wards for mixed-race population (8.5%), with significant numbers of residents born in the Americas and Caribbean (10.4%) or in Africa (11.7%). A majority of households here are socially rented (52.5%). This area is part of the Vauxhall and Camberwell Green constituency, where there was no serious challenge in 2024 to Labour MP Florence Eshalomi. She was first elected in 2019 for the predecessor Vauxhall seat, and before then she was a Lambeth councillor and sat as the London Assembly member for Lambeth and Southwark. Eshalomi has recently joined the government ranks as a junior minister in the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local and Government. Like Holborn and St Pancras, this is a left-wing part of London where the Green Party put up a strong challenge in May. The Lambeth Greens generally fared better than the Camden Greens, and they became the largest party in May with 29 seats against 26 Labour and 8 Lib Dem councillors; a minority Green administration was eventually formed. One of the Green councillors had been disendorsed by the party during the campaign for anti-Semitism and quickly resigned, and the Greens lost the resulting by-election in Streatham St Leonard's ward to the Liberal Democrats; a further by-election on the same day saw the Greens hold off a Labour challenge in Clapham Park ward. In May 2026 Myatt's Fields turned in a very close result, with 39% for the Greens and 36% for Labour. Those two parties ended up splitting the ward's two seats between them, with Labour councillor Annie Gallop holding her seat just 17 votes ahead of the second Green candidate. Labour now have a by-election of their own to defend here after Annie Gallop died in August, following several years of cancer treatment. Gallop was a long-serving councillor who was first elected for Vassall ward in 2014 before transferring to Myatt's Fields in the 2022 boundary changes; in her working life she had been a pub landlady in Oval and chair of the tenants' organisation for the Cowley estate within the ward. Gallop was mayor of Lambeth in 2021-22, and during her mayoral year she raised £7,000 for - appropriately enough, given her surname - the Ebony Horse Club in Brixton. Defending for Labour is Molly Hartill who was their losing candidate in May; she runs the office of a Labour MP. The Greens have selected Sonia Winifred, who is a fairly recent recruit to their cause: Winifred is a long-serving Labour councillor for Knight's Hill ward who was first elected in May 2014. On that occasion Winifred was disqualified on the grounds that she worked for Lambeth council; she then resigned her job, stood for re-election in the resulting August 2014 by-election and won. Also on the ballot are Christopher French for the Lib Dems, Mark McKee for Reform UK, John Hindson for the Conservatives, Yemi Awolola for the Christian Peoples Alliance and Anya Krycek for the Socialist Party of Great Britain. Kilburn, Denby, Holbrook and Horsley

Amber Valley council, Derbyshire; caused by the resignation of Conservative councillor Julie Whitmore. We now travel north into the East Midlands for a ward with a rather cumbersome name, most of which is having its third by-election in less than two years. Kilburn, Denby, Holbrook and Horsley ward covers the four parishes of those names, which lie generally between Belper to the west and Heanor to the east with the A38 trunk road linking the area to Derby. Kilburn, which lies on the Belper-Heanor road, gets top billing as the largest of the four parishes. The parish of Denby at the north end of the ward is best known as the birthplace of John Flamsteed, the first Astronomer Royal, and for the Denby Pottery Company which has been firing pottery products here for over two centuries.

All this lies within the Amber Valley parliamentary seat, a key marginal which since its creation in 1983 has changed hands whenever the government changes. It was a Labour gain in 2024 for Linsey Farnsworth, a solicitor who had a previous local government base in this area. Farnsworth had previously been elected to Amber Valley council in 2023 as the Labour councillor for this ward, with the other two seats going to the Conservatives: vote shares were 37% for Labour, 34% for the Conservatives and 12% for the Green Party.

Reform UK had put up an unusually strong challenge for that year in the 2023 Amber Valley elections after picking up a number of Conservative defectors, but in the end this had the effect of splitting the right-wing vote and resulted in a large Labour majority being elected: Labour won 26 councillors against 7 Conservatives, 6 Greens, 2 Belper Independents and a single Lib Dem. The Labour majority in the district has since disappeared as a result of four by-election losses and some defections on top, and Labour now run Amber Valley as a minority with 19 out of 42 seats. One of those by-election losses came in Kilburn, Denby, Holbrook and Horsley ward, after Labour councillor Linsey Farnsworth resigned following her election to Parliament in 2024. The resulting by-election was held on the undercard of the 2025 Derbyshire county council election and it was gained by the Conservatives, on a rather low vote share of 32%; the Green Party came second on 25%, Labour fell to 22% and third place, while fourth place on 14% went to independent candidate Alex Stevenson who had previously been a long-serving Conservative councillor for the former Shipley Park, Horsley and Horsley Woodhouse ward. Stevenson had defected to Reform UK in advance of the 2023 elections but lost his Amber Valley council seat to the Greens at that election, and he was the Reform UK candidate for Amber Valley at the 2024 general election, but by now he had fallen out with them.

That by-election in May 2025 had no Reform UK candidate, and things might otherwise have been different. On the same day the Horsley county division was gained by Reform UK, with vote shares of 35% for RUK, 27% for the Greens, 23% for the Conservatives and 10% for Labour. Horsley covers three of the four parishes in this ward, while Holbrook parish is part of the South Belper and Holbrook division which voted for the Green Party; Matt Murray, who had won the district by-election for the Conservatives, simultaneously finished in a poor third place in that county seat.

The voters of Denby, Horsley and Kilburn parishes were then called to the polls again in January this year for a Derbyshire county council by-election, after Horsley division's Reform UK county councillor Richard Morgan resigned on health grounds. This by-election ended up being gained by the Green Party, who polled 44% of the vote to 35% for Reform UK and 14% for the Conservatives. Horsley county division also covers Smalley, Shipley and Horsley Woodhouse ward which voted Green in 2023. Labour have nominated first-time candidate Andrew Bayton. Town

Newcastle-under-Lyme council, Staffordshire; caused by the resignation of Reform UK councillor Christine Duffy. In the last section we noted that the four-element ward name of "Kilburn, Denby, Holbrook and Horsley" was accurate but unweildy. UK ward names tend to be shorter than that, and you can't get much shorter than the four letters of Town ward in Newcastle-under-Lyme. A name like that should be foolproof - but something that is foolproof hasn't yet encountered the fool that can mess it up. The town of this ward name - and, for reasons which will become clear, we do need to explain this - is Newcastle-under-Lyme, which the locals would rather you did not bracket with the Potteries. Despite the fact that its boundary with nearby Stoke-on-Trent cannot be discerned on the ground, Newcastle-under-Lyme is not a pottery town. Its traditional industries were textiles and coal-mining, while the town of Newcastle-under-Lyme also benefitted from its position on the main road between Manchester and Birmingham.

The eponymous "new castle", of which no trace remains today, was founded here in the Anarchy of the 12th century. A market town grew up around it, which became prosperous and important enough to send two MPs to Parliament from 1354 onwards. The Newcastle-under-Lyme constituency has existed ever since, although it was reduced to one MP by the 1885 redistribution.

From 1906 until his elevation to the peerage in 1942 the MP here was Josiah Wedgwood IV, who served as Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster in the first Labour government. Wedgwood also served as mayor of Newcastle-under-Lyme in 1930-31 and he successfully fought off an attempt by Stoke-on-Trent to annex the town; s "postcard poll" of Newcastle-under-Lyme's residents showed overwhelming opposition to this idea. Local government reorganisers, take note.

The Newcastle-under-Lyme constituency has normally continued in the Labour vein ever since Wedgwood's day, although it turned in a very close result in 2017 when Paul Farrelly was re-elected as the town's Labour MP by just 30 votes over the Conservatives, 21,124 to 21,094. Well, those were the declared figures; but in 2017 Newcastle-under-Lyme's electoral services department had been seen all its experienced full-time staff leave without being replaced, and the mix of consultants, secondees, temps, part-timers and agency workers who ended up trying to administer that election made a series of rookie errors which ended up costing at least 998 and probably more like 1,500 people their votes (Andrew's Previews 2017, page 374). If the Conservatives had applied to the Election Court, they would almost certainly have had the 2017 election here voided and rerun.

As it was, the Conservatives had to wait until 2019 for Aaron Bell to become the first Tory MP elected for Newcastle-under-Lyme since 1880. Bell had come to politics from a career in the gambling industry with a side-hustle in TV quiz shows: he is the only person to have won both Only Connect and The Krypton Factor, and he was on the team from St John's College, Oxford which finished as runner-up in the 2000-01 series of University Challenge. One of his UC team-mates described Bell at the time as a "sport and politics guru, who would later vary between the brilliant and the inexplicable", which is not a bad summing-up of Bell's subsequent political career: a brilliant win in the 2019 general election was followed by what seemed to be an inexplicable last-minute retirement in 2024. Matters became clearer when in October 2024, after he had left the Commons, Bell was reprimanded by Parliament's Independent Expert Panel over a 2023 incident of drunken sexual misconduct in the Strangers' Bar. The Conservatives could not defend Aaron Bell's seat following his retirement and Newcastle-under-Lyme now has a Labour MP again, with Adam Jogee successfully transferring here in 2024 from his previous post on Haringey council. The Labour leader of Newcastle-under-Lyme council was forced to resign in 2017 once the details of how the council had mishandled that year's general election became clear, and the Conservatives took minority control with the support of independent councillors; the independents then joined the Conservatives to give the administration a majority, which was confirmed by the electorate at the 2022 election. Throughout this period Town ward in Newcastle-under-Lyme had stayed in the Labour column. This is - let's just be clear again - Newcastle-under-Lyme's town centre ward and it is noticeably younger than the rest of the town, thanks to the prescence of a large number of Keele University students. This column discussed all this at a by-election in January 2025 triggered by the resignation of Labour councillor Wendy Brockie one day before she would have been disqualified for not attending any meetings in six months; Brockie's seat was actually declared vacant under that rule before her resignation letter was found a few days later in a rarely-checked email folder. The resulting by-election to Newcastle-under-Lyme council in Newcastle-under-Lyme's Town ward returned Labour candidate Sheelagh Casey-Hulme with 40% of the vote, against 29% for the Conservatives and 22% for Reform UK. Since then Reform UK have run rampant in Newcastle-under-Lyme. In May 2025 the rather oddly-drawn Newcastle South county division, which covers the vast majority of Town ward which - for those who might still be confused - covers the town centre of Newcastle-under-Lyme, fell to Reform UK as part of their landslide on Staffordshire county council. Reform UK followed up in the 2026 Newcastle-under-Lyme council election by winning an overall majority, 27 seats against 15 Conservatives and just two seats for Labour. The two remaining Labour councillors were Dave Jones in the grossly-undersized Keele ward, which is dominated by the university and suffers from chronic under-enrolment because three-quarters of its population are students, and Sheelagh Casey-Hulme who topped the poll in Newcastle-under-Lyme's Town ward. Vote shares here in May were 28% for Labour, 27% for Reform UK, 22% for the Greens and 16% for the Conservatives. The other councillor for this ward appears to have been elected by mistake. Reform UK's Christine Duffy, who defeated her running-mate for Newcastle-under-Lyme's Town ward's second seat by 14 votes, appears to have under the impression that she was standing for election to Kidsgrove town council, where she has served before. The local press reported in May that once Duffy was informed she had won she turned up to the Kidsgrove town council offices to accept her new office and sign the paperwork - only to be told by the town clerk that she wasn't a Kidsgrove town councillor, she was a Newcastle-under-Lyme borough councillor representing an area which she had previously complained was favoured in council spending decisions at the expense of her local patch in Kidsgrove. The farcical confusion did not end there. In August Duffy decided to resign her councillor role on the grounds that she had received "very little or no training for this role". But, inexplicably, her resignation letter was sent to Kidsgrove town council, and it took nearly two weeks for the town clerk to forward it on to the borough council offices in Newcastle-under-Lyme. By this point Duffy was having second thoughts about resigning, but she eventually agreed to leave Newcastle-under-Lyme borough council citing health grounds. This forces the second by-election is two years for Town ward - and, let's just be clear one more time, this is the Town ward of Newcastle-under-Lyme council covering Newcastle-under-Lyme town centre. Defending for Reform UK is Adam Griffiths, who was elected last year as the county councillor for the Newcastle South division which covers this ward; in May he was an unsuccessful candidate for Westbury Park and Northwood ward. The Labour candidate is retired firefighter and former borough councillor Andrew Fox-Hewitt, who represented Bradwell ward from a December 2017 by-election until he was defeated in May. The Greens have reselected Mark Jones who was on their slate for this ward five months ago. Standing for the Conservatives is Conna Eynon who last fought this ward in 2022; in May he stood in Crackley and Red Street ward. Morgan Inwood completes an all-male ballot paper for the Lib Dems. Midstocket/Rosemount

Aberdeen council, Scotland; caused by the death of Scottish National Party councillor Bill Cormie. We'll travel up to the city of Aberdeen for today's final by-election. Midstocket/Rosemount is the city's inner west ward, stretching out along the Westburn Road and the Midstocket Road from the edge of the city centre as far as the Lang Stracht crossroads. This ward is the city's healthcare centre, taking in the Aberdeen Royal Infirmary which is the major hospital for north-east Scotland; also here are the Royal Cornhill psychiatric hospital and a number of other healthcare units. Open spaces here include Midstocket Park at the western end of the ward, Westburn Park at its centre and Union Terrace Gardens at the south-east corner. Midstocket/Rosemount ward was created in 2007 and returns three Aberdeen councillors by proportional representation. All four ordinary elections here to date have returned one councillor each from the Conservatives, Labour and the SNP, although the balance has been disturbed by a couple of previous by-elections. The Conservative councillor elected in 2007 died very soon afterwards and the SNP gained the resulting by-election, which was the first by-election held in Scotland under the Alternative Vote following the introduction of the PR system for local elections. A later by-election in 2015 saw a further SNP by-election from the Conservatives, but both of those gains were reversed at the following ordinary election. Vote shares here at the 2022 election were 38% for the SNP, 25% for the Conservatives and 21% for Labour. For Westminster elections the ward is mostly within the Aberdeen North constituency, which has been held since 2015 by the Scottish National Party's Kirsty Blackman. The parts of the city centre included in the ward are, however, covered by the Aberdeen South constituency which the Conservatives' Douglas Lumsden gained from the SNP at a by-election in June. The rest of this ward last went to the polls in May's Scottish Parliament election. At Holyrood Midstocket/Rosemount is entirely within the Aberdeen Central constituency which was taken over in May by SNP MSP Jack Middleton, a former special adviser to John Swinney. Finishing in fifth and last place in this constituency was the Liberal Democrat candidate Yi-pei Chou Turvey, who got into Holyood nonetheless as a regional MSP for North East Scotland; she is currently seeing out the final year of her term as an Aberdeenshire councillor. This third Midstocket/Rosemount by-election is triggered by the death in August of Bill Cormie, who had been the ward's SNP councillor since 2007. He was 71, and he had previously served as the council's depute provost. Cormie would have won a single-seat contest in this ward in 2022, although only narrowly: after transfers his margin over Labour would have been 51.1-48.9. Defending this seat for the SNP is Jeff Diack, a former radio DJ who now works as a tour guide and funeral celebrant. Not at the same time, hopefully. The Conservative candidate Freddie John is a former Aberdeen councillor, who represented the neighbouring ward of Hilton/Woodside/Stockethill from 2017 until he lost his seat in 2022. Another former Aberdeen councillor on the ballot is Labour's Willie Young who represented Bridge of Don ward from 2007 to 2017; Young subsequently appeared in this column when he contested a 2019 by-election in Torry/Ferryhill ward. The Scottish Green candidate is Lauren Bird; the Lib Dems have nominated Sandy Stephen; Simon McLean, who is a regular independent candidate in Aberdeen council elections, is back for another go; Reform UK will be hoping that there can be only one and that their candidate John McLeod will receive The Prize; and Kusum Singal, who describes herself as a researcher and educator, is an independent candidate. This is a Scottish local by-election, so Votes at 16 and the Alternative Vote apply here.
