Five by-elections on 8th October 2026, starting with the big one:

Holborn and St Pancras

House of Commons; caused by the resignation of Labour MP Sir Keir Starmer. London - then defined as the area of the Metropolitan Board of Works - was the biggest winner in seat terms from the 1885 redistribution, which made single-member constituencies the norm for the first time. Middlesex alone went up from 18 MPs to 47, and altogether the MBW area was divided into 59 constituencies. This figure goes to show just how densely populated inner London was then. In 1885 Holborn was a constituency of its own (one of three seats covering the parliamentary borough of Finsbury), and St Pancras was divided into four constituencies called St Pancras East, North, South and West. Those names are positively imaginative compared with the wards drawn up for St Pancras borough council on its creation in 1900: those wards didn't have names at all, instead being numbered 1 to 8 from north to south. All four St Pancras constituencies voted Liberal at their first election in 1885, before swinging more or less in tune with the national tide. In its first incarnation St Pancras North covered western Kentish Town and Tufnell Park, together with Gospel Oak and Highgate which are no longer in this constituency. Its first MP was Thomas Bolton, a Grays Inn solicitor, who defeated the Conservatives' Charles Cochrane-Baillie by 55-45. Cochrane-Baillie then won the rematch in 1886. In 1890 Charles Cochrane-Baillie succeeded to his father's titles and entered the House of Lords as the second Lord Lamington, also inheriting a large estate in Lanarkshire. Thomas Bolton then narrowly got his seat back at the resulting first St Pancras North by-election of 4th March 1890, defeating the new Conservative candidate Henry Graham by 108 votes - a majority which reduced further to 60 votes in 1892. Lord Lamington then spent significant amounts of time out of the UK as Governor of Queensland in 1896-1901 and then Governor of Bombay in 1903-07, and his first governorship left a culinary delicacy: the lamington cake, a popular Australian dessert consisting of sponge cake coated with chocolate sauce and coconut, is reputed to be named after him. After defecting to the Liberal Unionists in or around 1893, Thomas Bolton retired at the 1895 general election at which St Pancras North was gained by the Conservatives' Edward Moon. It took until the 1906 landslide for the Liberals to get the seat back with their candidate Willoughby Dickinson, a Parliamentary draftsman who had been chair of the London County Council in 1900-01. In his first term of office Dickinson was a strong supporter of women's suffrage and he introduced two bills on the subject. Following his re-elections by easy margins in both 1910 elections (his Conservative opponent in January was the wonderfully-named Hercules Pakenham, who would later serve in the Northern Ireland Senate) Dickinson sat on the Speaker's Conference on electoral reform, which recommended giving the vote to women, and he was sworn of the Privy Council in 1914. The St Pancras East constituency of 1885-1918 ran along the Midland railway line from Kentish Town to Somers Town, with its boundaries being defined by the Euston Road, Eversholt Street, Camden High Street, Kentish Town Road, Leighton Road and York Way. Large amounts of its acreage which taken up by goods depots for the Great Northern Railway and the Midland Railway, whose Kings Cross and St Pancras termini were part of the seat. East was narrowly won at its first election in 1885 by the Liberals' Thomas Gibb, the vestry clerk of St Pancras. Gibb was rather soundly defeated in 1886 by the Conservatives' Robert Webster, a barrister who wrote a number of books on subjects including world trade and the law relating to canals - which must have been helpful for his time in the Commons, given the presence of the Regent's Canal in his constituency. After leaving Parliament Webster wrote a 1907 book with the title of Elections, Electors and Elected: Stories of Elections Past and Present, which I must try and get hold of a copy of. Robert Webster resigned from the Commons in 1899 to go back to his legal career. The resulting St Pancras East by-election was narrowly held for the Conservatives by Thomas Wrightson, who returned to the Commons after losing his seat in Stockton in 1895: he held off the Liberal candidate Benjamin Costelloe, a London county councillor for Bethnal Green South East, by 187 votes. Wrightson was re-elected more comfortably in 1900, and he was also made a baronet in that year. In 1906 Sir Thomas Wrightson was defeated in the Liberal landslide by Hugh Lea, a newspaper proprietor who sttod down after one term. Lea stood down after one term and passed St Pancras East on to Joseph Martin, who had previously had a long political career in his native Canada. Martin was briefly prime minister of British Columbia in 1900, represented Winnipeg in the Canadian House of Commons from 1893 to 1896 and also sat for nine years in the Legislative Assembly of Manitoba. He was also a member of the Canadian Bar, and he had come to the UK to practise before the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council which was then the final court appeal for Canada. He won St Pancras East easily enough in both 1910 elections, but then fell out with his constituency party and fought off a deslection attempt in 1914 by threatening to resign his seat and stgand in the resulting by-election as an independent Lib-Lab candidate. Martin ended up defecting to Labour, and he stood as a Labour candidate in Islington in 1918 after St Pancras East disappeared in boundary changes. Meanwhile the St Pancras West constituency was dominated by the London and North Western Railway, taking in the passenger terminus at Euston and the large goods depot at Primrose Hill. Camden Town, which was much more industrial at this time, was also part of the seat together with the eastern fringe of Regent's Park. In 1885 St Pancras West became the first of a large number of seats represented by the Liberals' Harry Levy-Lawson, who was just 23 at the time. He was from the family which owned and ran the Daily Telegraph, which was rather less right-wing in those days. Levy-Lawson narrowly held on to his seat in 1886 by 64 votes, before going down to defeat in 1892 by 42 votes. His next electoral contest, an October 1892 by-election in Cirencester, was even closer: Levy-Lawson lost by 3 votes to the Conservatives' Thomas Chester-Master and took the result to the Election Court, which after a recount and scrutiny ruled that the 1892 Cirencester by-election had ended with a tied result. All this was facilitated by St Pancras West voting in 1892 for the Conservatives' Harry Graham, who would go on to serve for 14 years before being swept away in the Liberal landslide. This brought to the Commons Sir William Collins, who had a prominent medical career as a surgeon despite holding antivax views which were unusual even then. Collins had been chairman of the London County Council in 1897-98 and was the first chair of the LCC's education commitee. He had been knighted in Edward VII's Coronation honours in 1902. During his first Commons term he became vice-chancellor of the University of London. The two 1910 elections in St Pancras West both finished on a knife-edge. Collins held his seat in January just 10 votes ahead of the Conservatives, before losing his seat in December by the even narrower margin of 9 votes. This brought into the Commons the Conservative MP Felix Cassel, a barrister who was the nephew of the banker and philanthropist Sir Ernest Cassel. Despite his German background - he had been born into a Jewish family in Cologne - Felix Cassel fought in the First World War on the British side before being appointed in 1916 as Judge Advocate-General, responsible for courts-martial in the Army. Cassel then left the Commons to take up his new role, and the resulting St Pancras West by-election of 16th October 1916 was won unopposed, thanks to the wartime political truce, by the Conservatives' Richard Barnett. A barrister by trade, Barnett was an Irishman from County Down who had competed in the shooting events at the 1908 London Olympics, finishing fourth in the 1,000 yard free rifle competition; he was also a strong chess player who had won the Irish championship in 1886. He was serving on the Western Front at the time of his election, and was mentioned in despatches in 1917; Barnett finished the war with the rank of captain. The tiny but densely populated St Pancras South constituency of 1885-1918 covered the part of St Pancras borough south of the Euston Road, running from Fitzrovia in the west to the King's Cross Road to the east. Then, as now, the seat was dominated by University College London and its first MP was strongly connected with that university. In 1885 this was the last of several constituencies represented by Sir Julian Goldsmid, 3rd Baronet, who had previously represented Honiton in 1866-68 and then Rochester from 1870 to 1880. He was originally elected in St Pancras South for the Liberals, but in 1886 he defected to the breakaway Liberal Unionists over the issue of Irish home rule. Under his new political colours Sir Julian held St Pancras South until his death in 1896, by which point he was UCL's vice-chancellor. Following his 1880 defeat in Rochester, Goldsmid then tried to get straight back into Parliament by contesting a May 1880 by-election in Sandwich which was corrupt from top to bottom. The Election Court voided the result on Goldsmid's petition, and a subsequent Royal Commission found that over 900 of the 2,115 electors had taken bribes and over 100 people had given bribes. Parliament refused to issue a writ for a second Sandwich by-election, and the town was disenfranchised in 1885 as a punishment for its corruption. The St Pancras South by-election of 28th January 1896, following Sir Julian Goldsmid's death, was won by the new Liberal Unionist candidate Herbert Jessel who was Goldsmid's son-in-law. His father Sir George Jessel had risen to become Solicitor-General and Master of the Rolls, but Herbert had made his career as an Army officer before entering politics: before too long he was back in uniform serving in South Africa during the Boer War. Jessel was also a Westminster city councillor and he was mayor of Westminster in 1902-03. It took the 1906 Liberal landlside to lever Herbert Jessel out of office, and even then he only lost by 61 votes to Philip Wilson, a Cumbrian-born journalist who had been one of the first editors of Granta. Wilson only served on term, as Jessel comfortably regained St Pancras South for the Liberal Unionists in January 1910. The final piece of the puzzle in the 1885-1918 period was the even smaller Holborn constituency, which covered an area of just over 400 acres - even smaller than the City of London. The constituency was created in 1885 as one of three divisions covering the parliamentary borough of Finsbury, but in 1900 it became an independent local government unit as the smallest of the County of London's 28 metropolitan boroughs. Its population fell consistently during this period, from over 70,000 in 1885 to just under 25,000 in 1951. The Holborn constituency covered a diverse area between Westminster and the City, including two of the four Inns of Court (Lincoln's Inn and Gray's Inn), the jewellery quarter around Hatton Garden, the fashionable Georgian housing of Bloomsbury and the rather less fashionable slum housing of St Giles. It's fair to say that St Giles was consistently outvoted, because Holborn was a safe Conservative seat throughout its existence. Its first election set the tone: the Conservaives' Francis Duncan, a Royal Artillery officer, won with 62% of the vote and a majority of 1,574 votes over the Liberal candidate. Duncan increased his majority in 1886 when the Liberal candidate was Dadabhai Naoroji, who had just become one of the founding members of the Indian National Congress; Daoroji subsequently served as MP for Finsbury Central in 1892-95. Francis Duncan died in office in 1888. The resulting first Holborn by-election of 29th November 1888 was won by the Conservative candidate Gainsford Bruce, who finally made it into Parliament at his fifth attempt. He was a QC specialising in shipping law. Gainford Bruce left the Commons just after being re-elected in 1892, to become a High Court judge. The resulting second Holborn by-election of 12th August 1892, the first by-election of the 1892-95 Parliament, was won without a contest by Sir Charles Hall, a lawyer specialising in Admiralty matters who was naking a quick return to Parliament after being defeated in Chesterton at the general election. Hall also became Recorder of London in that year, an appointment which required him to resign as attorney-general to the future Edward VII. Nobody opposed his re-election to the Commons in 1895. Sir Charles Hall died in 1900 at the age of 56. The resulting third Holborn by-election of 23rd March 1900 returned James Remnant, yet another barrister who was a London county councillor for Holborn and chaired the LCC's theatre committee. He was unopposed at both the by-election and the 1900 general election shortly afterwards, setting up a 28-year career on the Conservative backbenches. Renmant was made a baronet in 1917, and eventually swapped the green benches for the red benches in 1928 when he was created as the first Lord Remnant. The resulting fourth Holborn by-election of 28th June 1928 exposed a split in the local Conservatives, with a large faction of the local party opposing the selection of Stuart Bevan as the new Conservative candidate and threatening to stand an independent candidate against him; however, no independent candidacy was forthcoming and Bevan won the by-election very comfortably. He was a bencher of the Middle Temple, and he became Recorder of Bristol in 1932. Stuart Bevan died in October 1935 at the age of 63, on the day that Parliament was dissolved for that year's general election. His seat was duly filled at the general election by Sir Robert Tasker, who - in a break from the pattern of Holborn's MPs being barristers - was an architect. Tasker was a long-serving London county councillor who was chairman of the LCC in 1930-31; he had previously sat in the 1924-29 Parliament as the Conservative MP for Islington East. Smewhat to his own surprise, Sir Robert Tasker was deselected for the 1945 general election in favour of Max Aitken, who was the son and heir of Lord Beaverbrook and his Daily Express newspaper empire. Aitken had spent the war as an RAF officer and ace fighter pilot, credited with 16½ kills and winning the DSO and DFC. He won Holborn by the unusually narrow margin of 925 votes over the Labour candidate Irene Marcousé, who shortly afterwards became the first Labour leader of Holborn council. The Holborn constituency had survived the 1918 redistribution more or less intact, but that year's boundary changes had reduced St Pancras borough from four MPs to three. The seat which disappeared was St Pancras South, which got divided between the East and West constituencies to create new St Pancras South East and South West seats. In the case of St Pancras South West, the Fitzrovia area - then covered by St Pancras ward number 7 - was a straightforward addition to the old St Pancras West. The Conservatives' Richard Barnett, who had taken over St Pancras West in a wartime 1916 by-election, easily achieved re-election in South West in 1918 with the government coupon and went on to win three further terms of office in the 1922-24 general elections; he was helped in 1923 by the left-wing vote being evenly split between the Liberals and Labour. Barnett was knighted in 1925. Sir Richard Barnett retired at the 1929 election, in which St Pancras South West voted Labour for the first time: the Conservative candidate Patrick Spens, who would later serve as the last chief justice of British India, lost by 1,779 votes to Labour's William Carter who was a railway union official - a helpful connection in a seat which included Euston. Carter was also a St Pancras councillor and he had served as mayor of the borough in 1919-20. William Carter was swept away in 1931 on a huge swing to the Conservatives of over 21%, which delivered a crushing win for the Conservative candidate George Mitcheson. Mitcheson was re-elected rather more narrowly in 1935, and was knighted shortly afterwards in the 1936 New Year honours for political and public service. He retired from the Commons in 1945. St Pancras South East had a more complicated history. Compared to the old St Pancras East, it gained the King's Cross area (ward 8) but lost Kentish Town (the part of ward 3 north of the Camden Road), which was transferred to St Pancras North. Its first contest in 1918 was equally complicated, with all four candidates achieving the 12½% required to save their deposits amid some confusion as to who, if anyone, had the government coupon. In the end the winner was the Conservatives' John Hopkins, a civil engineer, with second place going to the Liberal candidate Richard Reiss, third place to independent candidate Percy Adams who had secured the Liberal nomination for the old St Pancras East in 1917 but was then displaced by Reiss on his return from the war, and fourth place went to Labour candidate Herbert Romeril. Romeril moved into second place in St Pancras South East in 1922, before defeating John Hopkins at the 1923 general election by 692 votes. He worked at the Railway Clearing House, located just outside the constituency boundary on Eversholt Street near Euston station, where he was a union official. In the 1924 election Romeril lost his seat back to the Conservatives' John Hopkins, but he returned to the Commons in 1929. The 1931 Conservative landslide saw St Pancras South East gained by Sir Alfred Beit, 2nd Baronet, who came from a South African mining family: on the death of his father the previous year Sir Alfred had inherited not only a baronetcy but also a large fortune and art collection. Beit was narrowly re-elected in 1935 ahead of Labour candidate Santo Jeger, and he was still young enough to join the Royal Air Force when the Second World War broke out and serve in Bomber Command. The expanded St Pancras North swung in the same way as St Pancras South East, and the Conservative win here in 1918 was facilitated by the Tory candidate John Lorden getting the coupon ahead of the outgoing Liberal MP Sir Willoughby Dickinson, who was defeated. Lorden was a Wandsworth councillor who had twice served as mayor of that borough, in 1903-04 and 1907-08, and during the First Wold War he had chaired a section of a local military tribunal. He was re-elected in 1922 at the top of a three-way marginal result. Lorden was defeated at the 1923 general election by Labour's James Marley, a Scotsman who had moved to London to take up a teaching position in Hampstead. Marley served only one term before losing his seat in 1924 to the Conservatives' Ian Fraser. Fraser lost his seat back to Marley in 1929, but got it back in the 1931 Tory landslide and was re-elected for a third term in 1935. Ian Fraser was a businessman who had lost his sight to a German bullet on the Somme, and after the war he had become chairman of St Dunstan's, the charity for blind ex-servicemen: he would go on to hold that position for over half a century. Fraser was knighted in 1934 for his work at St Dunstan's. He combined his business career with a long political career - many years later, Fraser was the first person to receive a life peerage under the Life Peerages Act 1958 - but this was temporarily interrupted in 1936 when Fraser was appointed to the BBC board of governors. This required him to leave the Commons. The resulting second St Pancras North by-election of 4th February 1937 was narrowly won by the defending Conservative candidate Robert Grant Ferris, who was an officer in the Royal Auxiliary Air Force. He served in the senior RAF during the Second World War, and he was one of the first airmen to receive the Air Efficiency Award on its creation in 1942. In the same year Ferris added a hyphen to his name by deed poll, becoming Robert Grant-Ferris. All three St Pancras constituencies were gained by Labour in the 1945 Attlee landslide, with Robert Grant-Ferris losing his seat to George House. House was a former secretary of the Constructional Engineering Union, and he also represented St Pancras North on the London County Council. George House died in 1949 at the age of 56. The resulting third St Pancras North by-election of 10th March 1949 was won comfortably enough by Labour candidate Kenneth Robinson, an insurance broker and St Pancras councillor who had served in the Royal Navy during the Second World War, rising in rank from ordinary seaman to lieutenant-commander in five years. The 1945 election in St Pancras South East was a rematch of the 1935 contest between the Conservative MP Sir Alfred Beit and Labour candidate Santo Jeger, a doctor who had spent many years in local government before entering the Commons. Jeger had been first elected to Shoreditch council in 1925, serving as mayor of Shoreditch in 1929-30, and he had sat on the London County Council since 1931. He had provided medical aid to the republican side in the Spanish civil war. St Pancras South West was gained in the 1945 election by Labour's Haydn Davies, a Welshman who had come to London to work as a teacher. Many years previously, in 1929, Davies had contested St Pancras South West as the Liberal candidate. He served only one term in the Commons before being displaced by boundary changes, and he ended up seeking re-election in 1950 in York without success. The population of Central London in general fell significantly in the first half of the 20th century, as improved transport links made commuting into central London from the outer suburbs easier while slum clearance - whether municipally led, or of the more radical type effected in the 1940s by the Luftwaffe Town Planning Department - reduced the density of housing in the centre. By the time of the 1950 redistribution - the first redrawing of the parliamentary boundaries since 1918 - London had become severely over-represented due to a combination of all these factors. The opportunity was taken to abolish or merge a large number of inner-city seats, and Holborn and St Pancras did not escape this process. The combined area of the two boroughs went down from four MPs to two, with the abolition of St Pancras South East and St Pancras South West. The southern parts of those seats (covering Regsnt's Park, Euston, Somers Town and the railway termini) were added to the Holborn constituency to create a new seat called Holborn and St Pancras South, while St Pancras North expanded to take in the Camden Town and Camden Square areas. The 1950 boundary changes turned St Pancras North into a safe Labour seat and set the 1949 by-election winner Kenneth Robinson up for a long career in Parliament. When Labour came to power in 1964 Robinson joined the Wilson Cabinet as minister of health, serving in that position for four years; in 1968 he was reshuffled to become minister for planning and land before leaving the frontbench in 1969. Robinson had long campaigned on health and mental health issues, and his four years as minister of health saw the negotiation of a GPs' charter and the passage of the Abortion Act 1967, which legalised abortion in Great Britain by registered practioners on certain grounds and made abortion available on the NHS. He had also campaigned for suicide to be decriminalised, which eventually happened in 1961, and he was a supporter of the Wolfenden report which had recommended the decriminalisation of gay sex between consenting adults. Robinson had also served as chairman of the National Association for Mental Health, the charity now known as Mind, and this led to a conflict with Scientologists after he denounced them in Parliament as a cult: the Church of Scientology responded by publishing defamatory articles about Robinson, for which they ended up having to pay him substantial libel damages. Kenneth Robinson retired from the Commons in 1970 and passed the St Pancras North seat on to the new Labour MP Albert "Jock" Stallard, a Camden councillor who had also served as an alderman and councillor on the previous St Pancras council. Stallard was a government whip for most of the Callaghan government, when Labour had little or no majority in the Commons. In both 1974 elections his Conservative opponent was a thirtysomething banker from Lambeth called John Major, whose February 1974 campaign was his début as a Parliamentary candidate. The Holborn and St Pancras South seat was more closely contested from its creation in 1950, but Labour always had the edge here and the Conservatives only won the seat once, in the Macmillan landslide of 1959. Its first MP was Labour's Santo Jeger, who took the expanded seat over after previously representing St Pancras South East from 1945; of the other predecessor seats, Haydn Davies of St Pancras South West uncessfully contested York as already stated, while Holborn's Conservative MP Max Aitken retired; Jeger ended up with a majority of 1,230 votes over the Conservatives in 1950. In 1951 the Conservatives put up the former Nottingham MP Louis Gluckstein, who was a political giant in the sense that he was at the time the tallest person ever elected to the Commons at 6 feet and 8 inches; Santo Jeger increased the Labour majority, against the national trend. Santo Jeger died in 1953 at the age of 55. The resulting Holborn and St Pancras South by-election of 19th November 1953 saw a further swing to Labour, who selected his widow Lena Jeger: she was a London county councillor and St Pancras councillor, who worked as a journalist for what was then still the Manchester Guardian. Lena increased the Labour majority to 1,976 votes, and she was more narrowly re-elected for a full term in 1955 as one of the very few women MPs in the Commons at the time: of the 630 MPs elected in 1955, only 24 were women. For the 1959 election in Holborn and St Pancras South the Conservatives selected Geoffrey Johnson-Smith, a former London county councillor who had a high public profile at the time as a TV presenter. Shortly before the election Johnson-Smith had been promoted to temporary co-host of the BBC's Tonight programme, deputising for the show's regular presenter Cliff Michelmore who was recovering from a hernia operation. On election night his BBC colleagues got to report the election of one of their own: in a straight fight Johnson-Smith defeated Lena Jeger by 17,065 votes to 16,409, a Conservative majority of 656. In his first term in Parliament Geoffrey Johnson-Smith successfully piloted a private member's bill, the National Assistance (Amendment) Act 1962, which enabled local councils to provide meals-on-wheels services. But his majority was too small to expect a long parliamentary career in Holborn and St Pancras, and Lena Jeger got her seat back in 1964 with a majority of 2,576. Johnson-Smith got back into a Parliament the following year by winning a by-election in East Grinstead, and that seat did set him up for a long parliamentary career: he eventually retired in 2001. Lena Jeger went on to be re-elected four more times in Holborn and St Pancras, combining her MP role from 1968 with membership of the Labour Party NEC. She eventually retired in 1979 and passed what become a safe Labour seat on to Frank Dobson. Dobson had previously served from 1973 to 1975 as leader of Camden council, which had replaced Holborn, St Pancras and Hampstead councils in 1965, before standing down from the council to take up a politically restricted job with the Local Government Ombudsman. Following further boundary changes in 1983 Frank Dobson became the first MP for the present Holborn and St Pancras constituency, which was created to reduce the number of MPs for Camden from three to two. This resulted in the dissolution of the St Pancras North constituency, with Highgate being added to the Hampstead constituency (which was renamed Hampstead and Highgate) while the rest of the St Pancras North seat was transferred into Holborn and St Pancras South to create a seat with similar boundaries to those of today, the main difference being that the 1983 seat included Gospel Oak. Highgate was transferred into the Holborn and St Pancras seat in 2010, but this change was reversed in 2024. To date Holborn and St Pancras has been a safe Labour seat which has only had two MPs in the last 43 years. Frank Dobson took the expanded seat over in 1983 and used it as a springboard for his long frontbench career: he was a fixture of the Labour Shadow Cabinet from 1987 onwards, and took over the high-profile position of health secretary in Tony Blair's first cabinet in 1997, serving in Cabinet for two years. In 2000 Dobson was the Labour candidate in the first election for mayor of London, finishing in third place between the Conservatives' Steve Norris and the winning independent candidate Ken Livingstone, whom Dobson had controversially defeated for the Labour nomination. Frank Dobson eventually retired from the Commons in 2015, having served for 36 years, and the Labour nomination to succeed him went to Sir Keir Starmer who was coming rather late to elected politics - he was aged 52 at the time. Appropriately for a seat containing two of the Inns of Court, Starmer had made his career in the law: he was a barrister specialising in human rights cases, and he then served from 2008 to 2013 as Director of Public Prosecutions, heading the Crown Prosecution Service. This was effectively a senior civil service role, and following his retirement Starmer became a knight of the Order of the Bath - an honour which is traditionally reserved for senior civil servants and high-ranking military officers - in the 2014 New Year Honours list. In May 2015 he was elected as Labour MP for Holborn and St Pancras with 53% of the vote, against 22% for the Conservatives and 13% for the then leader of the Green Party, Natalie Bennett. With such a prominent previous CV, it was only natural that Sir Keir Starmer would quickly rise to the Labour frontbench. In September 2015 he was appointed by Jeremy Corbyn as shadow immigration minister, and he then served in the Shadow Cabinet from October 2016 onwards as shadow Brexit secretary - staying in that role throughout the turbulent years of the 2017-19 Parliament which were dominated by that single issue. Following the Labour defeat at the 2019 general election and the resignation of Jeremy Corbyn as party leader, Sir Keir Starmer won the 2020 Labour leadership election with 56% of the vote against Rebecca Long-Bailey and Lisa Nandy, and he served as leader of the opposition through the rest of the COVID-hit 2019-24 Parliament. Once the Conservatives' polling position had disintegrated through a combination of controversy and chronic incompetence, it was only a matter of time before Sir Keir became Prime Minister - which he did following the massive Labour win in the 2024 general election. The Starmer government proved to be a disappointment for many of its supporters, and Sir Keir eventually resigned from the top job two years in rather than face a leadership challenge from Andy Burnham following the latter's win in June's Makerfield by-election. Once Parliament reconvened following the summer recess, Sir Keir Starmer tendered his resignation from the Commons. Rumour has it that he is interested in a new job on the world stage, with the Secretary-Generalship of NATO having been mentioned. We can see a hint of what was to come for the Starmer administration in Sir Keir's personal result in Holborn and St Pancras in 2024. He won very comfortably with 49% of the vote in a field of 12 candidates, and a numerical majority of 11,572 votes; but party leaders usually get a boost in their own constituency at general election time, and that didn't happen here. After taking account of boundary changes, Starmer's percentage score fell by around 17 percentage points from the 2019 election. Most of this fall was to the benefit of candidates to the left of the Labour Party. Second place in Holborn and St Pancras in 2024 went to Andrew Feinstein, who polled 19% of the vote standing as a left-wing and pro-Gaza independent candidate; third place went to the Green Party on 10%.
