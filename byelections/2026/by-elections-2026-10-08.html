Five by-elections on 8th October 2026, starting with the big one:

Holborn and St Pancras

House of Commons; caused by the resignation of Labour MP Sir Keir Starmer. London - then defined as the area of the Metropolitan Board of Works - was the biggest winner in seat terms from the 1885 redistribution, which made single-member constituencies the norm for the first time. Middlesex alone went up from 18 MPs to 47, and altogether the MBW area was divided into 59 constituencies. This figure goes to show just how densely populated inner London was then. In 1885 Holborn was a constituency of its own (one of three seats covering the parliamentary borough of Finsbury), and St Pancras was divided into four constituencies called St Pancras East, North, South and West. Those names are positively imaginative compared with the wards drawn up for St Pancras borough council on its creation in 1900: those wards were numbered 1 to 8 from north to south. All four St Pancras constituencies voted Liberal at their first election in 1885, before swinging more or less in tune with the national tide. In its first incarnation St Pancras North covered western Kentish Town and Tufnell Park, together with Gospel Oak and Highgate which are no longer in this constituency. Its first MP was Thomas Bolton, a Grays Inn solicitor, who defeated the Conservatives' Charles Cochrane-Baillie by 55-45. Cochrane-Baillie then won the rematch in 1886. In 1890 Charles Cochrane-Baillie succeeded to his father's titles and entered the House of Lords as the second Lord Lamington, also inheriting a large estate in Lanarkshire. Thomas Bolton then narrowly got his seat back at the resulting first St Pancras North by-election of 4th March 1890, defeating the new Conservative candidate Henry Graham by 108 votes - a majority which reduced further to 60 votes in 1892. Lord Lamington then spent significant amounts of time out of the UK as Governor of Queensland in 1896-1901 and then Governor of Bombay in 1903-07, and his first governorship left a culinary delicacy: the lamington cake, a popular Australian dessert consisting of sponge cake coated with chocolate sauce and coconut, is reputed to be named after him. After defecting to the Liberal Unionists in or around 1893, Thomas Bolton retired at the 1895 general election at which St Pancras North was gained by the Conservatives' Edward Moon. It took until the 1906 landslide for the Liberals to get the seat back with their candidate Willoughby Dickinson, a Parliamentary draftsman who had been chair of the London County Council in 1900-01. In his first term of office Dickinson was a strong supporter of women's suffrage and he introduced two bills on the subject. Following his re-elections by easy margins in both 1910 elections (his Conservative opponent in January was the wonderfully-named Hercules Pakenham, who would later serve in the Northern Ireland Senate) Dickinson sat on the Speaker's Conference on electoral reform, which recommended giving the vote to women, and he was sworn of the Privy Council in 1914. The St Pancras East constituency of 1885-1918 ran along the Midland railway line from Kentish Town to Somers Town, with its boundaries being defined by the Euston Road, Eversholt Street, Camden High Street, Kentish Town Road, Leighton Road and York Way. Large amounts of its acreage which taken up by goods depots for the Great Northern Railway and the Midland Railway, whose Kings Cross and St Pancras termini were part of the seat. East was narrowly won at its first election in 1885 by the Liberals' Thomas Gibb, the vestry clerk of St Pancras. Gibb was rather soundly defeated in 1886 by the Conservatives' Robert Webster, a barrister who wrote a number of books on subjects including world trade and the law relating to canals - which must have been helpful for his time in the Commons, given the presence of the Regent's Canal in his constituency. After leaving Parliament Webster wrote a 1907 book with the title of Elections, Electors and Elected: Stories of Elections Past and Present, which I must try and get hold of a copy of. Robert Webster resigned from the Commons in 1899 to go back to his legal career. The resulting St Pancras East by-election was narrowly held for the Conservatives by Thomas Wrightson, who returned to the Commons after losing his seat in Stockton in 1895: he held off the Liberal candidate Benjamin Costelloe, a London county councillor for Bethnal Green South East, by 187 votes. Wrightson was re-elected more comfortably in 1900, and he was also made a baronet in that year. In 1906 Sir Thomas Wrightson was defeated in the Liberal landslide by Hugh Lea, a newspaper proprietor who sttod down after one term. Lea stood down after one term and passed St Pancras East on to Joseph Martin, who had previously had a long political career in his native Canada. Martin was briefly prime minister of British Columbia in 1900, represented Winnipeg in the Canadian House of Commons from 1893 to 1896 and also sat for nine years in the Legislative Assembly of Manitoba. He was also a member of the Canadian Bar, and he had come to the UK to practise before the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council which was then the final court appeal for Canada. He won St Pancras East easily enough in both 1910 elections, but then fell out with his constituency party and fought off a deslection attempt in 1914 by threatening to resign his seat and stgand in the resulting by-election as an independent Lib-Lab candidate. Martin ended up defecting to Labour, and he stood as a Labour candidate in Islington in 1918 after St Pancras East disappeared in boundary changes. Meanwhile the St Pancras West constituency was dominated by the London and North Western Railway, taking in the passenger terminus at Euston and the large goods depot at Primrose Hill. Camden Town, which was much more industrial at this time, was also part of the seat together with the eastern fringe of Regent's Park. In 1885 St Pancras West became the first of a large number of seats represented by the Liberals' Harry Levy-Lawson, who was just 23 at the time. He was from the family which owned and ran the Daily Telegraph, which was rather less right-wing in those days. Levy-Lawson narrowly held on to his seat in 1886 by 64 votes, before going down to defeat in 1892 by 42 votes. His next electoral contest, an October 1892 by-election in Cirencester, was even closer: Levy-Lawson lost by 3 votes to the Conservatives' Thomas Chester-Master and took the result to the Election Court, which after a recount and scrutiny ruled that the 1892 Cirencester by-election had ended with a tied result. All this was facilitated by St Pancras West voting in 1892 for the Conservatives' Harry Graham, who would go on to serve for 14 years before being swept away in the Liberal landslide. This brought to the Commons Sir William Collins, who had a prominent medical career as a surgeon despite holding antivax views which were unusual even then. Collins had been chairman of the London County Council in 1897-98 and was the first chair of the LCC's education commitee. He had been knighted in Edward VII's Coronation honours in 1902. During his first Commons term he became vice-chancellor of the University of London. The two 1910 elections in St Pancras West both finished on a knife-edge. Collins held his seat in January just 10 votes ahead of the Conservatives, before losing his seat in December by the even narrower margin of 9 votes. This brought into the Commons the Conservative MP Felix Cassel, a barrister who was the nephew of the banker and philanthropist Sir Ernest Cassel. Despite his German background - he had been born into a Jewish family in Cologne - Felix Cassel fought in the First World War on the British side before being appointed in 1916 as Judge Advocate-General, responsible for courts-martial in the Army. Cassel then left the Commons to take up his new role, and the resulting St Pancras West by-election of 16th October 1916 was won unopposed, thanks to the wartime political truce, by the Conservatives' Richard Barnett. A barrister by trade, Barnett was an Irishman from County Down who had competed in the shooting events at the 1908 London Olympics, finishing fourth in the 1,000 yard free rifle competition; he was also a strong chess player who had won the Irish championship in 1886. He was serving on the Western Front at the time of his election, and was mentioned in despatches in 1917; Barnett finished the war with the rank of captain. The tiny but densely populated St Pancras South constituency of 1885-1918 covered the part of St Pancras borough south of the Euston Road, running from Fitzrovia in the west to the King's Cross Road to the east. Then, as now, the seat was dominated by University College London and its first MP was strongly connected with that university. In 1885 this was the last of several constituencies represented by Sir Julian Goldsmid, 3rd Baronet, who had previously represented Honiton in 1866-68 and then Rochester from 1870 to 1880. He was originally elected in St Pancras South for the Liberals, but in 1886 he defected to the breakaway Liberal Unionists over the issue of Irish home rule. Under his new political colours Sir Julian held St Pancras South until his death in 1896, by which point he was UCL's vice-chancellor. Following his 1880 defeat in Rochester, Goldsmid then tried to get straight back into Parliament by contesting a May 1880 by-election in Sandwich which was corrupt from top to bottom. The Election Court voided the result on Goldsmid's petition, and a subsequent Royal Commission found that over 900 of the 2,115 electors had taken bribes and over 100 people had given bribes. Parliament refused to issue a writ for a second Sandwich by-election, and the town was disenfranchised in 1885 as a punishment for its corruption. The St Pancras South by-election of 28th January 1896, following Sir Julian Goldsmid's death, was won by the new Liberal Unionist candidate Herbert Jessel who was Goldsmid's son-in-law. His father Sir George Jessel had risen to become Solicitor-General and Master of the Rolls, but Herbert had made his career as an Army officer before entering politics: before too long he was back in uniform serving in South Africa during the Boer War. Jessel was also a Westminster city councillor and he was mayor of Westminster in 1902-03. It took the 1906 Liberal landlside to lever Herbert Jessel out of office, and even then he only lost by 61 votes to Philip Wilson, a Cumbrian-born journalist who had been one of the first editors of Granta. Wilson only served on term, as Jessel comfortably regained St Pancras South for the Liberal Unionists in January 1910. The final piece of the puzzle in the 1885-1918 period was the even smaller Holborn constituency, which covered an area of just over 400 acres - even smaller than the City of London. The constituency was created in 1885 as one of three divisions covering the parliamentary borough of Finsbury, but in 1900 it became an independent local government unit as the smallest of the County of London's 28 metropolitan boroughs. Its population fell consistently during this period, from over 70,000 in 1885 to just under 25,000 in 1951. The Holborn constituency covered a diverse area between Westminster and the City, including two of the four Inns of Court (Lincoln's Inn and Gray's Inn), the jewellery quarter around Hatton Garden, the fashionable Georgian housing of Bloomsbury and the rather less fashionable slum housing of St Giles. It's fair to say that St Giles was consistently outvoted, because Holborn was a safe Conservative seat throughout its existence. Its first election set the tone: the Conservaives' Francis Duncan, a Royal Artillery officer, won with 62% of the vote and a majority of 1,574 votes over the Liberal candidate. Duncan increased his majority in 1886 when the Liberal candidate was Dadabhai Naoroji, who had just become one of the founding members of the Indian National Congress; Daoroji subsequently served as MP for Finsbury Central in 1892-95. Francis Duncan died in office in 1888. The resulting first Holborn by-election of 29th November 1888 was won by the Conservative candidate Gainsford Bruce, who finally made it into Parliament at his fifth attempt. He was a QC specialising in shipping law. Gainford Bruce left the Commons just after being re-elected in 1892 to become a High Court judge. The resulting second Holborn by-election of 12th August 1892, the first by-election of the 1892-95 Parliament, was won without a contest by Sir Charles Hall, a lawyer specialising in Admiralty matters who was naking a quick return to Parliament after being defeated in Chesterton at the general election. Hall also became Recorder of London in that year, an appointment which required him to resign as attorney-general to the future Edward VII. Nobody opposed his re-election to the Commons in 1895. Sir Charles Hall died in 1900 at the age of 56. The resulting third Holborn by-election of 23rd March 1900 returned James Remnant, yet another barrister who was a London county councillor for Holborn and chaired the LCC's theatre committee. He was unopposed at both the by-election and the 1900 general election shortly afterwards, setting up a 28-year career on the Conservative backbenches. Renmant was made a baronet in 1917, and eventually swapped the green benches for the red benches in 1928 when he was created as the first Lord Remnant. The resulting fourth Holborn by-election of 28th June 1928 exposed a split in the local Conservatives, with a large faction of the local party opposing the selection of Stuart Bevan as the new Conservative candidate and threatening to stand an independent candidate against him; however, no independent candidacy was forthcoming and Bevan won the by-election very comfortably. He was a bencher of the Middle Temple, and he became Recorder of Bristol in 1932. Stuart Bevan died in October 1935 at the age of 63, on the day that Parliament was dissolved for that year's general election. His seat was duly filled at the general election by Sir Robert Tasker, who - in a break from the pattern of Holborn's MPs being barristers - was an architect. Tasker was a long-serving London county councillor who was chairman of the LCC in 1930-31; he had previously sat in the 1924-29 Parliament as the Conservative MP for Islington East. Smewhat to his own surprise, Sir Robert Tasker was deselected for the 1945 general election in favour of Max Aitken, who the son and heir of Lord Beaverbrook and his Daily Express newspaper empire. Aitken had spent the war as an RAF officer and ace fighter pilot, credited with 16½ kills and winning the DSO and DFC. He won Holborn by the unusually narrow margin of 925 votes over the Labour candidate Irene Marcousé, who shortly afterwards became the first Labour leader of Holborn council.
