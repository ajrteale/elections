"All the right votes, but not necessarily in the right order"

Before we start this week's edition of Andrew's Previews, there are some announcements which need to be made.

First, we have now reached the second week of November, and - as long-term readers of this column will know - that means it is time to point out that the six-month rule is now in effect ahead of the next set of UK local elections in May 2027. And this is an almost entirely UK-wide set of local elections, with the whole of local government up for election in Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland. In England there will be elections to the vast majority of the metropolitan boroughs, most unitary councils and (subject to possible reorganisation) nearly all of the second-tier districts. Only Greater London, a few councils with unusual electoral cycles and some areas which have already been reorganised (including Cornwall, Dorset, Durham, Northumberland, Shropshire, Surrey and Wiltshire) will miss out on the fun.

In seat terms, 2027 promises to be the biggest set of UK local elections for decades. The flipside of this is that the level of council by-elections for the next six months or so will be unusually low, with so many councillors approaching the end of their terms. This week's menu consists of six local by-elections taking place in England outside London on 12th November 2026. On the basis of the previous results these are for three Labour seats, two independents and one Reform UK, but of course things are never that simple. Reform UK might well fancy their chances of a clean sweep this week, but in these by-elections they will face competition from their right in the form of Restore Britain who have a full slate of six candidates. We're going to start the week by boarding the clown car to the biggest basket case in local government right now...

Failsworth West

Oldham council, Greater Manchester; caused by the resignation of Mark Wilkinson.

If you want some tips on how to do local government effectively, for God's sake do not take the Metropolitan Borough of Oldham as your model. The home of the tubular bandage has had a toxic political culture for a very long time now, and following the May 2026 local elections the council has descended into a state of deadlock as personal and political grievances collided with electoral arithmetic.

Failsworth West ward lies in the borough's south-west corner only a few miles to the north-east of Manchester city centre, part of the urban sprawl within the M60 motorway. The main communication links between Manchester and Oldham - the A62 Oldham Road, the tram line, and the Roman road that came before them - all pass through Failsworth. The Oldham Road crosses the Rochdale Canal next to Failsworth Town Hall, which was the home of an independent urban district until 1972 when the town was incorporated into Oldham.

Failsworth has an unusually high White British population for a ward within the M60, which needs to be born in mind in looking at its voting patterns. The ward has often voted Labour this century, but the Conservatives broke through here in 2008. They didn't defend their seat in 2012, when the ward was gained by Labour candidate Sean Fielding. One of the other seats in Failsworth West was gained by UKIP in 2014, before reverting to Labour in 2018.

Sean Fielding, who by now had become the Labour leader of Oldham council, lost his seat in 2021 following an extraordinarily nasty campaign, which was dominated by a series of online conspiracy theories alleging that he had covered up Asian grooming gangs in Oldham. This column is not aware of any evidence to suggest that was true, and Fielding has since made a political comeback on Bolton council. The beneficiary of this was Mark Wilkinson, who stood on the ticket of the newly-formed Failsworth Independent Party and defeated Fielding by 46-40. The Failsworth Independent Party gained a second seat in the ward from Labour in 2022, bringing Sandra Ball to the council.

Oldham had a whole-council election on new ward boundaries in May 2023, by which point Wilkinson and Ball had fallen out with the Failsworth Independent Party and sought re-election as independent candidates. Ball was re-elected, but Wilkinson lost his seat to Labour. He got it back in 2024, defeating Labour by 48-34 again as an independent candidate.

Ball and Wilkinson subsequently joined Reform UK, who gained the last Labour seat in Failsworth West ward in May 2026 by a very convincing margin: Mark Ruthven polled 56% of the vote, to just 17% for the outgoing Labour councillor Peter Davis and 15% for the Green Party. This was one of 13 Oldham wards which voted for Reform UK in May, catapulting them into second place on the council. In the wake of the May elections, Labour's Arooj Shah resigned as council leader. Wilkinson, who had had a kidney removed two months earlier, became deputy leader of Oldham's Reform UK group.

The Oldham council website currently lists 18 Labour or Labour/Co-operative councillors, 14 for Reform UK, 10 for the Oldham Group (all but one of whom are from the Asian community and represent wards in Oldham town), six Lib Dems, four Conservatives, three independents, two Royton Independents and one remaining councillor for the Failsworth Independent Party, with two vacant seats. Nobody is anywhere near the 31 seats required for a council majority, and the council is so deeply split that four separate meetings since May have failed even to elect a leader. Since May the council officers have been trying to keep the show on the road under emergency powers.

Oldham council's monitoring officer attempted to force the issue in August by issuing a section 5 notice, pointing out that the council was breaking the law by operating without a leader or a cabinet (for example, there is a statutory requirement to have a named lead member for children's services) and calling an emergency full council meeting to resolve the issue or else. The EGM proceeded to vote down motions to install as council leader the Labour group leader Shah, the Reform UK group leader Lewis Quigg (who had previously been in the Conservatives and contested Failsworth West as a Conservative in a November 2012 by-election), the Oldham Group leader Kamran Ghafoor and the Lib Dem leader Sam al-Hamdani.

This column does not use the Clown Car GIF lightly. It is reserved for the very worst cases of dysfunctional councils.

Somewhat surprisingly, central government have not reacted to this by sending in the Commissioners. Instead, a compromise of sorts was finally cooked up under which the Labour, Reform UK, Oldham Group and Lib Dem group leaders would each become "interim lead members" for certain council services. Let's be clear: the officers are still using emergency powers of dubious legality to run the show.

In order to get this far Lewis Quigg had to be forced out as leader of the Reform UK group, with Mark Wilkinson taking over as group leader and interim lead member for children's services on 18th September. Six days later Wilkinson went to the Royal Oldham Hospital, where he was diagnosed with a heart condition and instructed to "take things easy and get plenty of rest". He promptly resigned as a councillor on health grounds, and this by-election is to replace him. Quigg subsequently quit Oldham council in a huff, and there will be a further by-election in December to fill his seat in Royton North ward.

The most recent poll here was the Greater Manchester mayoral by-election in August, for which a ward-level vote breakdown has been put together by Britain Elects' Ben Walker. This showed Labour back on top in Failsworth West, although only just: the Labour candidate Bev Craig polled 36% of the first preferences to 35% for Reform UK and 15% for Restore Britain. This was a margin of 18 votes which was easily overturned in the runoff - after transfers Failsworth West voted for a Reform UK mayor over Labour by 51.6-48.4 - but there will be no runoff in this by-election.

Since 2024 Failsworth has been part of the Manchester Central parliamentary seat, which is not as bizarre as it sounds: the city centre is only a few miles away. This seat is represented by the deputy leader of the Labour Party Lucy Powell, who serves in Andy Burnham's cabinet as education secretary. Before then Failsworth had been included in the Ashton-under-Lyne constituency from 1997 to 2024: this was latterly the seat of another Labour deputy leader, Angela Rayner.

This by-election will be recorded by the Local Elections Archive Project as a gain from independent, reflecting that Mark Wilkinson had been last elected as an independent candidate before joining Reform UK. There is no independent candidate in this by-election, and the Failsworth Independent Party appear to have thrown in the towel. In practice the defending Reform UK candidate is Chris Baddeley, a self-employed businessman and British Legion standardbearer. Labour have reselected Pete Davis, who lost his seat here in May: Davis has previously represented Failsworth West ward from 2018 to 2022 and 2023 to 2026. Also returning from May's election here is the Green Party candidate Andrew Jones. A ballot paper of six candidates is completed by Lynne Thompson for the Lib Dems, Stephen Davenport for the Conservatives and Bob Gunn for Restore Britain.

Hindley Green

Wigan council, Greater Manchester; caused by the death of independent councillor Bob Brierley.

Take the road from Atherton towards Hindley and Wigan, and you will reach a point identified on the map and on the Bee Network bus stop signs as "Dangerous Corner". This is a name which promises more than it delivers. The road junction itself is not obviously more dangerous than any other T-junction. Meanwhile Corner Lane, the main road leading up to the junction from the west, is notable for its conspicuous lack of corners: it is the only part of the Roman road between Manchester and Wigan which is still in use as a road today.

Corner Lane takes travellers from Dangerous Corner into Hindley Green, a classic pit settlement on the Lancashire coalfield which has now merged into a single urban area with Hindley to the west. The ground here has been very extensively undermined, and in 1852 there were no fewer than fourteen collieries in operation in Hindley Green.

Further along the main road, the traveller towards Wigan will pass on the left the private residence of 370 Atherton Road, also known as Hindley House, which dates from the 18th century and is listed at Grade II. The listing concentrates on the house's decoration and doesn't mention its most famous resident: in the early 1900s the Booth family moved in here and got their son George apprenticed as a jockey. Ultimately young George followed his father into the entertainment business and became a household name. We know him as George Formby junior.

Formby was never a political animal, but when he toured South Africa in 1946 - just before apartheid was brought in - he refused to play racially-segregated venues. Legend has it that when the South African National Party leader D F Malan (who went on to introduce apartheid) telephoned George Formby to complain about him performing to black audiences, Formby's wife and manager Beryl Ingham replied "Why don't you piss off, you horrible little man?"

George Formby wouldn't have been able to perform to black audiences of this size back in Hindley Green, even in the modern day. The 2021 census put the ward's non-white population at 3.2%, and recorded only 38 black residents of the ward (0.3%). Hindley Green is in the top 100 wards in England and Wales for Christianity (66.2%), which is a common feature of the census return for Lancashire past and present.

With the exception of a very small area around the Leigh Road/Smiths Lane junction which was transferred into this ward from Leigh West in 2023, Hindley Green is part of the Makerfield parliamentary seat. This was the focus of national attention over the summer just gone, after its Labour MP Josh Simons resigned in order to allow Andy Burnham a path back into Parliament. Burnham duly won the resulting by-election in June, and he is now Prime Minister.

This result wasn't a given, as we can see from the May 2026 elections to Wigan council in which Reform UK swept 24 of the 25 seats up for election: the one that got away was Atherton North, won by an Independent Network candidate. Hindley Green was typical of the turquoise tide, with Reform UK enjoying a 52-33 lead over Labour.

In previous years Hindley Green had been a regular tussle between Labour and long-serving independent councillor Bob Brierley, whose death in September at the age of 67 prompted a tribute from Prime Minister Burnham. Brierley was first elected for this ward in 2004 on the ticket of Community Action, which was the main opposition to Labour on Wigan council at the time. By 2007 Community Action had split and Brierley lost re-election on the ticket of the splinter group Community Performance First, but he returned to the council in 2008 as an independent candidate and had served the residents of Hindley Green ever since. Labour currently hold the remaining seat in Hindley Green ward, and they did well enough in the 2023 and 2024 elections that they still hold a majority on Wigan council despite the 2026 wipeout.

The Prime Minister has already had one electoral test in his constituency, which was the Greater Manchester mayoral by-election in August. This would seem to indicate that Labour are back in the ascendancy here. The ward-level breakdown shows that in Hindley Green ward Burnham's successor Bev Craig led the Reform UK candidate Sian Astley by 47-32 in the first round, narrowing to 55-45 in the runoff.

Bob Brierley was due for re-election in May 2027, so whoever wins this by-election to replace him will not be able to rest on their laurels for long. One new independent candidate has come forward to replace him: local resident Mark Prescott was runner-up to Labour here in 2024 and also fought this ward in 2023. Reform UK have selected Bonnie Clarke, who also gives an address in the ward. Another local on the ballot is Labour's John Vickers, who was a councillor for this ward from 2019 until he lost his seat in May. The candidate list here is completed by Nat Smith for the Greens, Daniel Jackson for the Conservatives, David Burley for the Lib Dems and Restore Britain's Rebecca Shepherd, who had been the first-ever candidate for that party when she finished third in June's Makerfield by-election.

Huncoat

Hyndburn council, Lancashire; caused by the death of Labour councillor David Parkins.

For the last of our polls in historic Lancashire we have come to the northern slopes of the West Pennine Moors. Huncoat is a former pit village which lies to the north-east of Accrington. Much of the coal extracted here was consumed by Huncoat power station, a coal-fired plant which generated electricity from 1952 to 1984; most of the power station buildings have since been demolished, although the electrical substation remains in operation. Not far away from the old power station site is Huncoat railway station, which is on Northern Trains' East Lancashire line.

The village of Huncoat was incorporated into Accrington in 1929, and the modern Huncoat ward also includes the Hillock Vale area along the Burnley Road which is effectively a north-eastern extension of Accrington: this is where the town's cemetery is. Meanwhile up on the hill next to the A56 bypass is Huncoat Quarry, which is still in operation.

The census return shows that Huncoat ward is still strongly dependent on manufacturing. It's also a very white area - something which is important to understand in the context of East Lancashire, whose towns are often quite strongly racially segregated. Accrington has a large Asian community, but the town's Asian population is concentrated in Central and Spring Hill wards.

Hyndburn is one of the last examples of a council which has thirds elections but doesn't have a predominant pattern of three-member wards. There are 35 Hyndburn councillors, who are currently split 16 Labour plus this vacancy against 8 Conservatives, 8 Reform UK, a Green and an independent, with the latter two councillors banding together to form the "Progressive Hyndburn" group and generally supporting the Labour minority administration. However, the council has 16 wards with most wards (including Huncoat) returning two councillors, so to make the thirds system work only 11 or 12 wards hold elections in any given year with the other 4 or 5 having a year off. In Hyndburn the choice of which wards have the year off is done alphabetically, so Huncoat had its last fallow year in 2024 alongside Clayton-le-Moors, Immanuel and Milnshaw wards; next May Netherton, Peel, St Andrew's and Spring Hill wards will miss out on the fun.

The eight Reform UK councillors were all returned in May 2026 when the party won eight of the eleven seats up for election: the other three seats split evenly between the Conservatives, Labour and an independent. Huncoat was part of the turquoise tide, with Reform UK polling 49% of the vote here in May against 24% for Labour and 17% for the Green Party. The ward is divided between the Accrington North and Accrington South divisions of Lancashire county council, both of which were Reform UK gains last year (from Labour and the Conservatives respectively).

The Hyndburn council area is slightly smaller than the Hyndburn parliamentary seat, which also takes in the Rossendale town of Haslingden to the south-east. This seat was a Labour gain in the 2024 general election, with former Blackpool councillor Sarah Smith defeating one-term Conservative MP Sara Britcliffe.

Local elections in Huncoat have been dominated for many years by David Parkins, who died in September at the age of 76. Parkins was first elected for Huncoat ward in 1994 and served until 2006 when he stood down after 12 years as a Labour councillor. He then returned to Hyndburn council a year later as an independent councillor for Huncoat, being re-elected in 2011 on the ticket of a short-lived party called "Independents in Hyndburn". Parkins then retired in 2015 with his seat returning to Labour, and he then reconciled with the Labour party, being elected in 2018 as a Labour councillor for Huncoat once again. He retired in 2022 and then returned in 2023 for his seventh and final term of office. Away from politics Parkins had served for five years in the Royal Navy and for twelve years in the Army, including three tours of Northern Ireland, and he had worked for Hyndburn council before entering the council chamber. He was mayor of Hyndburn in 2001-02, and the council had voted to award him the freedom of the borough shortly before his death.

David Parkins had polled almost 80% of the vote at his final re-election in 2023, so the winner of this by-election has a lot to live up to - and not a lot of time to do it, because they will need to seek re-election in May. It has to be said that the by-election campaign has not been edifying. The defending Labour candidate is Jackie Rawstron, who is partially sighted and uses a white stick to get about. Hyndburn Labour had put up a picture of Rawstron and her canvassing team on their social media, to which Reform UK Hyndburn councillor Joel Tetlow posted a comment describing her as a "Stevie Wonder lookalike".

Facing a storm of "ableist" criticism for this, Tetlow did not apologise. Not only that, he then trawled through Rawstron's social media and found a number of similarly bad-taste comments which she had made about trans people, fat people and a referee in a Burnley FC match. Apparently "Stevie bloody Wonder could see it wasn’t a free kick". This prompted Labour to disendorse Rawstron, but unfortunately for them her place on the ballot paper as a Labour candidate was already signed, sealed, delivered.

This doesn't necessarily mean Labour are out of this contest: Rawstron is the only left-wing option on the ballot paper, and we saw plenty of cases in May where candidates won despite being disendorsed for stuff like this or worse. Nonetheless the Reform UK candidate Anna Stanislawka, who contested Immanuel ward in Oswaldtwistle in May, must be very happy with how this campaign has turned out. The Greens have not returned for this by-election, so the ballot is completed by Shahed Mahmood for the Conservatives (who won here as recently as 2022) and Mark Conlon for Restore Britain.

Cheddleton

Staffordshire Moorlands council; caused by the resignation of Labour councillor Dylan Pascall.

We now exchange the rolling hills of East Lancashire for the rolling hills of the Staffordshire Moorlands. Cheddleton is a large village which lies to the south of Leek, and it has significantly expanded in recent years with the development of St Edward's Park: this is a new housing estate built on the site of a former psychiatric hospital, which had closed in 2002. Many of the asylum buildings have been preserved including the impressive water tower, which is now a private residence.

Nearby Leek is considered to be the southernmost of the Pennine textile towns, and the parish church at Cheddleton (dedicated to St Edward the Confessor) is the final resting place of Sir Thomas Wardle, a local businessman who was knighted by Queen Victoria for his contribution to Leek's silk industry. Wardle owned a number of dyeworks in Leek, and he collaborated with a number of designers including William Morris: Morris returned the favour by contributing to the restoration of St Edward's, and much of the church's stained glass is by artists from the Morris group. Sir Thomas' wife (and also his cousin) Elizabeth Wardle was the driving force behind the Leek Embroidery Society, which produced a full-size replica of the Bayeux Tapestry in 1886: this is now on permanent display in Reading Museum, in case you can't get to the real thing while it's at the British Museum.

Modern-day tourists who want to observe the countryside around Cheddleton could do worse than take a trip on the Churnet Valley railway, a preserved line based at Cheddleton which has been in operation since the 1990s. The ward boundary also includes the station at Leek Brook on the recently-opened CVR extension to Leek, but that station is only opened on special occasions. The railway line from Leek to Stoke-on-Trent runs along the northern boundary of Cheddleton ward: this line hasn't seen a train since 1988 but is officially described as "mothballed".

Cheddleton has something of a commuter demographic - Stoke-on-Trent isn't that far away - and the census return shows high levels of owner-occupation and an unusually high white population. These are ingredients for a high Reform UK vote, and in the 2025 Staffordshire county elections Reform UK had a big lead in the local Churnet Valley county division.

The last elections for other levels of government took place here before Reform UK's rise. The Staffordshire Moorlands parliamentary seat has been represented since 2010 by the Conservatives' Dame Karen Bradley, who served in the Theresa May cabinets as culture secretary and then Northern Ireland secretary; she now chairs the Commons home affairs select committee. Bradley's seat is unusual in that Staffordshire Moorlands was a Labour seat throughout the Blair and Brown governments: this is largely explained by boundary changes, as the 1997-2010 version of this seat included Kidsgrove which then had a significant Labour vote.

Dame Karen Bradley's predecessor as MP was Labour's Charlotte Atkins, who now sits on Staffordshire Moorlands council and was a Staffordshire county councillor until she lost her seat to Reform UK last year. The 2023 Staffordshire Moorlands elections returned a hung council with Labour as the largest party, on 24 seats to 22 Conservatives, 8 independents, 1 Green and 1 Lib Dem. Labour then formed a minority administration which remains in place, although defection losses mean that Labour have now fallen behind the Conservative group.

Despite its commuter demographic, Cheddleton ward is capable of returning Labour councillors in a good year or when the Tories don't field a full slate. In 2023 the ward split its three seats, with two going to the Conservatives and one to Labour's Dylan Pascall: topline vote shares were 32% for the Conservatives, 28% for Labour, 19% for independent candidates and 13% for the Greens.

Dylan Pascall has now moved away from this area to take up a new job working for a Labour MP in York, and he tendered his resignation last month to trigger what will be the only Staffordshire Moorlands by-election of this council term. Defending the seat for Labour is Martin Sadler, who gives an address in the nearby town of Cheadle and is a veteran of 22 years' service in the RAF as a photographic specialist: his military service included spells in Northern Ireland and Iraq. The Conservative candidate is local resident Rob Bateman, a former police officer and Scout leader who spends his spare time renovating Minis and rally driving. We have an independent candidacy from Sue Rogers, who also gives an address in Cheddleton. The Green Party candidate is Matthew Frith. Completing the ballot paper from the political right are Reform UK's Su Beardmore, who is the local county councillor, and Restore Britain's Karen Pickering.

Croft Baker

North East Lincolnshire council; caused by the resignation of Labour councillor Marian Jervis.

We now travel east to the seaside. The curiously-named Croft Baker ward covers the centre of a town which was named by Trainline in 2021 as the second-best seaside destination in the UK reachable by train. That's Cleethorpes, whose wide and sandy beach on the south bank of the Humber estuary has been attracting holidaymakers by train ever since the seafront railway station opened on Easter Monday in 1863.

Croft Baker ward doesn't include the railway station, but it does take in the nearby Cleethorpes Pier which opened in 1873 and has been through a number of owners in recent years. The present pier is much shorter than the original, as part of the pier was demolished in 1940 (to forestall a German invasion) and never reinstated. A much newer construction on the seafront is the Cleethorpes lifeboat station, which was rebuilt in 2022-25 and is kept busy by the volume of shipping to Hull, Grimsby and Immingham. The housing inland from the seafront - which is centred on Cleethorpes Cemetery - bears the normal census hallmarks of seaside resorts, with low qualification levels and relatively high levels of private renting.

Cleethorpes currently has a Labour MP following the 2024 general election, in which Cleethorpes town was moved into a renamed Great Grimsby and Cleethorpes constituency. Labour backbencher Melanie Onn returned to the Commons in 2024 after five years away, having previously represented Great Grimsby from 2015 to 2019.

The towns of Cleethorpes and Great Grimsby form a single conurbation, but it has only been since 1996 that the two towns have been governed together by North East Lincolnshire council. The present ward map here dates from 2003, with Croft Baker ward being descended from a previous Humberside county council division of the same name. This is the second time Croft Baker has featured in Andrew's Previews, which covered a previous by-election in July 2015: that was a Labour hold.

In the last two decades Croft Baker has returned councillors from all of the three main Westminster parties plus Reform UK, who won the most recent election here in 2026 very easily with 51% of the vote, against 20% for Labour and 14% for the Green Party. The other two seats in the ward are held by Labour, who gained those seats from the Conservatives in 2023 and 2024. The 2024 winner was Labour councillor Marian Jervis, who was subsequently diagnosed with cancer and resigned her seat in September on health grounds.

Reform UK won 14 of the 15 North East Lincolnshire seats up for election in May, but the thirds electoral system meant that this wasn't enough for an overall majority. Nonetheless the Reform UK group - which is now down to 13 councillors following a defection loss - is the largest party on North East Lincolnshire council and they have formed a minority administration, against 10 Conservatives, 10 Labour councillors, 3 Lib Dems and 4 independents.

The defending Labour candidate Janet Mager has a mountain to climb in defending this by-election, as she will be well aware: Mager was a very distant runner-up to Reform UK here in May. Meanwhile Reform UK have selected first-time candidate Melissa Grice. The Green Party's Leon Brown will be hoping to improve on his third-place finish here in May. A ballot paper of six candidates is completed by Stephen Harness for the Conservatives, independent candidate George Georgiou who has been standing in this ward on and off for many years to (so far) very little effect, and Restore Britain candidate Matthew Branson.

Bosmere

Suffolk county council; caused by the resignation of Reform UK councillor Gary Keogh.

Our final poll this week is in rural Suffolk, not far to the north-east of Ipswich. The Bosmere county division covers 13 parishes, none of which are called Bosmere: this name, as with several other Suffolk county divisions, calls back to an ancient Hundred of Suffolk. The Bosmere and Claydon Hundred took its name from a small lake just outside Needham Market, which is no longer included within this county division.

In practice the county division is dominated by the town of Needham Market, which lies in the Gipping valley between Ipswich and Stowmarket: it is a stop for trains between Ipswich and Cambridge. This is a mediaeval town which was based around the wool-combing industry, but Needham Market has never really recovered from the Great Plague which killed two-thirds of its inhabitants between 1663 and 1665.

The Bosmere county division was once represented by the Lib Dems' Ros Scott, who was promoted to the House of Lords in 2000 (as Baroness Scott of Needham Market) and was president of the Lib Dems from 2009 to 2011. Baroness Scott stood down from Suffolk county council in 2005 and was succeeded by the Lib Dems' Julia Truelove, who herself retired in 2017. At that 2017 election the Bosmere seat was gained by the Conservatives after many years of trying; shares of the vote were 45% for Anne Whybrow and 39% for the Lib Dems' Steve Phillips. Whybrow died a year later and the Conservatives' Kay Oakes narrowly held the resulting by-election in October 2018, finishing 21 votes ahead of Phillips (Andrew’s Previews 2018, page 373). A rematch of Oakes v Phillips in 2021 saw Oakes increase her majority.

The local authority here is Mid Suffolk council, which in 2023 became the first and still the only British local government district to elect a majority of Green Party councillors. The ward and county division boundaries don't match up, but the four wards wholly or partly in this division returned a total of three Green councillors, two Lib Dems and one Conservative in 2023. The 2024 Westminster boundary changes united this division within the Central Suffolk and North Ipswich constituency, whose MP Patrick Spencer was readmitted to the Conservative whip this year after being acquitted of sexual assault charges.

Following a botched attempt at local government reorganisation, the 2025 Suffolk county council elections eventually went ahead in 2026. Reform UK won a majority on the county council, with 41 out of 70 seats, and Bosmere division was part of that majority. In a result completely out of kilter with everything that had gone before, Reform UK's Gary Keogh won Bosmere with 33% of the vote; second place went to the Green Party on 29%, with the previous top two of the Conservatives and Lib Dems trailing some way behind on 19% and 13% respectively.

Gary Keogh submitted his resignation at the end of September citing work and family commitments, prompting the first by-election of this Suffolk county council term. Defending for Reform UK is Patrick Wegener, who stood in Cosford division in May and gives an address in Babergh district: he has previously worked on the railways and the London Underground and as a hotel manager. The Green Party have reselected Adria Pittock, who stood here in May but was their parliamentary candidate for Ipswich in 2024 and has previously been a regular Green candidate in Ipswich local elections: she last appeared in this column in April last year when she stood in a county council division for St John's division in the county town. Kay Oakes, who was the previous Conservative county councillor from the 2018 by-election until she lost her seat in 2026, is seeking to return. Another candidate from May coming back for another go is Attila Borzak of the Lib Dems; he gives an address in Needham Market. The Bosmere ballot is completed by Saffeen Yamulki for Labour and by Nigel Mills for Restore Britain.
